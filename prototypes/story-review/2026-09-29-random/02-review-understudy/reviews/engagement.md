# Engagement and Believability — Understudy

**Score: 4/5**. It is engaging overall, with clear stakes, a real antagonist in Hester herself, and an ending that pays off the ring. But her central turn from cruelty to mentoring happens in a single line, and the rescue at its peak is staged rather than earned.

## Findings

### 1. [major] Hester's pivot from "the knife" to coaching happens with no beat in between
- **Where:** Scene 2 to Scene 3
- **Quote:** "Hester hardens her jaw. She chooses the knife." (Scene 2) / "\"From the entrance of the emissaries,\" Hester says, her voice low, flat. \"Take it from 'Now breaks the iron girdle.'\"" (Scene 3)
- **Problem:** Moments after deliberately choosing to break Maisie, and just after shouting "A cheap usurpation, Roland!", Hester starts coaching her. The story shows her considering walking out, then cuts straight to the cue. This is the hinge of a transformation story, and the reader is left to guess what moved her.
- **Reader needed:** One interior beat or trigger (the dropped notebook, Roland's "forty souls," something in how Maisie stands) that turns Hester before she speaks the cue.

### 2. [minor] The trapdoor rescue is staged so the danger barely exists
- **Where:** Scene 3 (set up in Scene 1)
- **Quote:** "Across the wings, on the elevated backstage platform, the manual release lever stands upright" / "caught safely in the arms of the stagehands waiting below."
- **Problem:** Scene 1 carefully sets up the counterweight. But the lever that solves the problem only appears at the moment it's needed, and stagehands are already waiting below for a planned descent. The "broken ankle" peril and Hester's heroic lunge feel set up for effect, and the real test (does she let it happen?) turns out to be low-risk.
- **Reader needed:** The lever, and the fact that no one else is manning it, established before the moment of danger, so her choice carries real weight.

### 3. [minor] Scene 4's attempt to buy the role back pulls against Scene 3's epiphany
- **Where:** Scene 4 (against Scene 3)
- **Quote:** "The audience will not merely admire him; they will weep for him." (Scene 3) / "Tear up the notice, Roland. Give me closing night." (Scene 4)
- **Problem:** Hester has just seen that Maisie's Malcor is better and saved her. Then she spends her life savings to take the part away from her. A relapse can work, but nothing in the prose acknowledges it as one. The reader has to decide whether Scene 3's realisation meant anything.
- **Reader needed:** Some sign that Hester knows she is backsliding, or that closing night means something different to her now than simply reclaiming the role.

### 4. [minor] The final obstacle is a contract the reader never saw
- **Where:** Scene 4
- **Quote:** "The subsidy is contingent on Maisie Callow taking the lead. Your money cannot buy out a town council contract."
- **Problem:** Up to this point the problem was money. So when Hester offers exactly the money needed, the block that stops her is a civic subsidy that has never been mentioned. The door closes because of a rule invented on the spot, not because of pressure built up earlier.
- **Reader needed:** The council subsidy, or Crossbeck's interest in Maisie, planted in Scene 2 or Scene 3.

### 5. [minor] Roland's speech about the company's sacrifice is repeated almost word for word
- **Where:** Scene 2, repeated in Scene 3 and Scene 4
- **Quote:** "She did it so the company could pay the carters and the stagehands who have gone without wages for three weeks" / "forty souls who haven't seen silver in three weeks"
- **Problem:** The same facts (half salary, forty souls, three weeks unpaid) are told to Hester three times in three scenes. The pressure doesn't grow with each telling, and the Scene 3 opening sits still while it repeats the end of Scene 2.
- **Reader needed:** Each confrontation adding a new piece of information or a new cost, rather than restating the last one.

### 6. [nit] The sabotage threat in Scene 1 is never actively pursued
- **Where:** Scene 1 (against Scene 2 and Scene 3)
- **Quote:** "Hester will find the lever, the cue, the weight that will sabotage Maisie's performance and take back what is hers."
- **Problem:** The promised plot never becomes a plan. Hester only ever passively considers letting harm happen, so the threat looks bigger at the start than it turns out to be.
- **Reader needed:** At least one deliberate step toward sabotage, so that turning away from it costs her something.

## What works
- Maisie's outburst in Scene 2 reframes Hester's teaching as the injury, and raises the emotional stakes: "you struck the prompter's table with your ring. You told me it was sweet, that it was sentimental, that sweetness was poison in a tragedy."
- The ring groove from Scene 1 pays off in the ending, which is specific to this story and lands on a physical image, not a moral: "there is only the startling sensation of air moving across skin that has been covered for half a lifetime."
- Hester's revised notes give the handover a concrete action rather than a speech: "I had always insisted you pinch the vowels, clip the cadence short. It was wrong. It was my voice, not Malcor's."

## Out of lane
Hester's tenure swings between "eleven unbroken years" and "for thirty years", and Maisie's coaching between "six years" and "thirty years of theatrical precedence".
