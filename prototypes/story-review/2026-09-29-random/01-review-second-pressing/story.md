# Second Pressing

## Scene 1
<!-- card: scene_01_the_tainted_casks -->

The lower barrel cellar always held the damp chill of cut limestone, but this morning the air smelled wrong. It wasn't the clean, earthy sting of toasted oak and cellar mold; it was the raw, prickly vapor of vinegar.

Bruno Callow worked down the first rack without waiting for her. He wrenched the silicone bung from lot four, bent his thin neck, and drew a short, furious breath.

"Rot," he said. He dropped the stopper; it hit the wet duckboards with a dull slap. "Acetic acid. It's sharp enough to strip copper."

Tess Vance kept her hands tucked in her apron pockets, her fingers digging against her thighs. "That's impossible, Bruno. Those casks were stable on Monday. The secondary fermentation finished clean weeks ago."

"Smell it yourself." Bruno moved to the next cask, lot five, and jerked its bung free with a wet sucking pop. He didn't even lean in; the sharp, vinegar vapor was already blooming out of the bunghole. "And this one. Go on. Try it."

Tess took two paces forward and drew air. The prickle hit the back of her nasal passages instantly—volatile acidity at runaway bloom, stripping every trace of dark fruit out of the room.

He reached lot six, the third in the tier, and stripped that bung too. The identical acidic stink drifted into the aisle.

"Every single one," Bruno said, his voice flat, dropping like lead. "All twelve oak barrels. Across every separate lot, both tiers. Ruined."

"All twelve?" The cold in Tess's chest hardened. Separate vineyard parcels did not succumb to an acute bloom simultaneously overnight. "They don't share cellar lines. They have separate cooperage."

"They shared you," Bruno said. He turned on her, his heavy wool sleeves powdered with white cellar mold. "You performed the topping-up on Tuesday afternoon. Topping-up—replacing the wine lost to evaporation so air couldn't pocket in the headspace—is work a first-year cellar hand manages without souring an entire vintage. My signature is on your seasonal employment agreement, Vance. Spoilage ruled as worker negligence forfeits your back wages and kills any industry reference you ever ask me for."

The clause was real, and it meant walking out of the county blacklisted and penniless.

"I sanitized every line," she said, holding her voice rigid. "The siphon hose soaked in metabisulphite for forty minutes. The transfer pipe was flushed twice."

"Then explain twelve dead casks sitting in front of you."

Tess turned from him, walking toward the alcove near the drainage trench. Resting on a low wooden dolly was the glass topping carboy, a thirty-litre demijohn in a wicker jacket, half empty from Tuesday's run. This was the reservoir she had drawn from to fill every barrel to the brim.

She knelt and eased the tapered rubber bung from the carboy's mouth.

She leaned close, bracing for the familiar, acrid sulfur bite of cellar preservative that should have singed her nostrils.

No sulfur flared. Instead, a soft, cloying sweetness drifted from the glass mouth—the unpreserved fragrance of dessert pressings left exposed, sweet and flat, completely devoid of sanitizing chemical.

Tess looked at the smooth amber glass. The topping vessel had not held protected wine at all. Someone had substituted or doctored it, and she would have to prove it before Bruno closed the ledger on her name.

## Scene 2
<!-- card: scene_02_the_drained_tank -->

The corrugated roof of the upper press shed shuddered under the mountain draft, its galvanized seams whistling against the timber rafters. Clara Callow wiped grease from her knuckles with a rag that smelled faintly of paraffin and cold iron. Through the floorboards, Bruno's muffled shouting had finally ceased, leaving behind only the rhythmic drip of condensation along the copper pipes.

The hatch slammed back against the stone sill. Tess Vance climbed into the shed, her boots gray with cellar limestone and her sleeves rolled high over chalky forearms. She did not bother to knock or greet her; she crossed straight to the row of tool lockers bolted behind the hydraulic press.

"Where is the dosing unit?" Tess asked, her breathing short.

Clara let the rag drop onto the workbench. "Locked up. Where it stays when we aren't running fruit."

Tess gripped the brass hasp of Clara's narrow supply cabinet. The iron padlock held it fast, seated clean in the staple without a scuff on the paint. "You didn't sulphite the topping wine. The carboy in the cellar smelled like fresh grape pulp."

"Of course I didn't sulphite topping wine," Clara said, frowning as she turned toward her. "That isn't my job. The cellar keeps its own jugs. The potassium metabisulphite dispenser has been locked in my cabinet all week. The key has been on my belt loop since Monday."

Tess rattled the padlock once. The metal gave half an inch against the wood and clicked shut. The dispenser, a calibrated glass cylinder packed with dry preservative tablets, sat completely undisturbed behind the wire mesh.

"Then what was in that carboy?" Tess demanded.

Clara looked past her toward the far corner of the shed, where her small stainless-steel experimental tank sat raised on wooden blocks. It was a twenty-gallon holding vat she had filled with second-run dessert pressings—raw, unpasteurized, and rich with wild yeasts that needed immediate chilling to keep from turning volatile. She had clamped the drainage valve shut with a brass butterfly nut after Friday's press.

The nut was off. It lay resting on the rim of an empty bucket.

Clara stepped over the wash hoses, her stomach tightening as she tapped the side of the steel tank. The sound was hollow and tinny, echoing all the way to the conical base. She flipped the inspection hatch. Nothing remained inside except a sticky amber smear across the bottom curve and the sweet, heavy perfume of unprotected juice.

"It's gone," Clara said, her voice dropping.

"All of it?"

"Twenty gallons of raw dessert pressings," Clara whispered. She looked at the rubber transfer hose coiled neatly beneath the spigot, still damp at the nozzle. A cold fury prickled behind her eyes as the shape of it came clear. "Bruno. He couldn't stomach the short harvest numbers on the ledger. He took my experimental run and emptied it straight into the carboys to stretch the cellar volume."

Tess stood motionless, her jaw rigid. "He dumped live, unpasteurized must into finished barrels?"

"He thinks because sugar tastes good, nobody will notice the difference until bottling," Clara spat, ripping her apron free and throwing it onto the grease-stained bench. The thought of her brother quietly draining her work while she slept set her pulse hammering against her ribs. "He ruined my trials to pad his barrel counts, and now he's downstairs pretending it's an act of God. I am going to make him answer for it."

## Scene 3
<!-- card: scene_03_clipping_the_blame -->

Tess had to hurry to keep pace with Clara down the stone stairs, their boots striking the damp risers in uneven percussion. The chill of the lower cellar met them like cold well-water rising around their knees, dense with the sour bite of ruined alcohol.

Bruno stood where Tess had left him, a clipboard pinned against his chest under the lantern light. He looked up, his jaw tightening as Clara rounded the rack of twelve coopered casks.

"You took my tank," Clara said, not breaking stride until she was an arm's length from his chest. "You went into the upper shed, unlocked my line, and drained twenty gallons of unpasteurized dessert pressings."

Bruno blinked, his shoulder shifting back against an upright beam. "Clara, lower your voice. The cellar has enough problems today."

"The topping carboy," Clara cut in, pointing violently down at the thirty-litre carboy nested in its woven wicker jacket beside cask four. "Tess smelled it, and I checked my tank. The valve was dry. You siphoned off my experimental must because we were short on volume, didn't you? You took raw, active juice and threw it into the cellar reserve to pad out the harvest."

For a second, the lantern flame caught the wet shine of Bruno's eyes. Tess watched him brace for a shout, but the rage she expected never landed. Instead, his shoulders dropped an inch. A faint, almost imperceptible slackness washed through his face—not the shock of an innocent man caught unawares, but the strange, sudden relief of someone handed a lifeline.

"We were thirty percent under on the vintage yield," Bruno said. His voice was taut, defensive, but he did not deny the drain. "The cooperage cannot sit half-filled through the winter. The airspace alone would have oxidized every gallon in the room."

"You dumped raw sugar into finished lots!" Clara's voice cracked against the vaulted ceiling. "You know what unheated dessert must does without a sulphite dose! It turned every stave into an incubator for acetobacter. You didn't stretch the cellar, Bruno—you killed it with schoolboy negligence!"

"It was an operational gamble," Bruno snapped, his defensiveness hardening into an edge as he pivoted toward Tess. "A miscalculation on the cellar balance, yes. I misjudged the residual sugars. But I left clear instructions for cellar prep. Tess handled the hose. She poured the fill. Any cellar hand with two working eyes should have inspected the carboy, spotted the haze of bacterial film, and tested the carboy before topping up twelve separate barrels."

Tess stiffened. "You told me the topping reserve had already been stabilized and measured."

"Section four of your agreement covers basic diligence," Bruno said, tapping the clipboard with a thumb. "I made a harvest decision under pressure to protect our numbers. You executed it without looking. Clara, your must is gone, and the vintage is sour, but don't stand there and pretend this wasn't ruined by careless cellar execution."

Clara let out a bitter, shuddering laugh, shaking her head at him. To Clara, the shape of the disaster was clear: her brother had grown desperate, cut a reckless corner to puff up their low gallonage, and ruined everything through sheer, arrogant incompetence.

## Scene 4
<!-- card: scene_04_the_developer_contract -->

When Clara stormed out toward the road, Bruno did not follow her. He turned and climbed the damp stone steps back up toward the upper press shed, his tread heavy, leaving Tess no choice but to track him.

The press shed smelled of cold metal and lees. Bruno stopped by the timber worktable, leaning over the grease-penciled crush logs with his palms flat against the grain. Beside the logbook lay an unstamped manila folder, half-tucked beneath an iron scale weight. A blue seal showed beneath the flap.

Tess reached it before he could turn. She pulled the stiff pages free. It was a formal purchase and mortgage-assumption agreement from Highline Crest Resorts. The clause on the second page was underscored in pencil: the developer agreed to assume the estate's entire outstanding bank debt, contingent upon the immediate and permanent cessation of all commercial winemaking on the parcel before spring.

"Put that down, Tess," Bruno said, his voice flat, exhausted.

"The hail," Tess said, reading the acreage addendum. "In November. It didn't just clip the tops."

"It took fifty percent of the fruit off the hill," Bruno said, not looking at her. "Half the crop gone before the first basket came in. We were already drowning. The bank was calling the notes. Clara wanted to replant, buy bulk juice, scrape through another five years of bleeding money. She wouldn't listen. She was never going to sell while there was still good wine sleeping in those barrels."

"So you killed it," Tess said. The whole week snapped into focus. "You didn't siphon Clara's unpasteurized tank to stretch the blend. You needed an acetic infection that looked like an accident."

Bruno rubbed his jaw, where dark stubble shadowed his neck. "The pressings had already turned sour on the skins. Full of active mother. I poured five gallons straight into the topping carboy on Monday night. All it took was fifty milliliters in each bung-hole to inoculate the lot."

"And you left the sulphur dispenser locked inside her cabinet," Tess said, a cold certainty settling in her chest. "You made sure I couldn't dose the barrels with metabisulphite even if I caught the turn in time. You let Clara believe you were just a reckless fool who butchered the gallonage, because incompetent was safer than criminal."

"She'll take the buyout now," he said quietly. "There is no wine left to bottle. All twelve casks are vinegar. She can't argue with an empty cellar."

"And what about me?" Tess stepped closer, holding the legal document between them like a blade. "You cited Section Four. You were going to dock my final three months' pay, forfeit my wages, and sign a conduct report that would blackball me out of every valley cellar from here to the coast."

Bruno blinked, his mouth parting as if the thought of her wage had genuinely slipped his mind amid the collapse of the estate. "Tess, be reasonable. The developer's escrow won't disburse until—"

"You're going to write out my full seasonal severance right now," Tess said, her voice dropping into a hard, quiet register. "The full twelve hundred dollars, plus a clean letter of discharge certifying the cellar work was executed to standard without fault or negligence. In ink. Signed by your hand."

Bruno stared at the pages in her grip, his jaw working. "And if I don't?"

"Then Clara reads this contract before she reaches the highway," Tess said. "And the agricultural board gets the carboy, the crush logs, and my sworn statement on how the mother got into the wine. I don't care about your resort, Bruno. But you will not balance your ruin on my back."

Bruno looked down at the drafting table, the defiance slowly leaking out of his shoulders. He reached into his coat for his fountain pen.

## Scene 5
<!-- card: scene_05_the_ruined_vintage -->

The ink on the draft slip was barely dry when Bruno followed Tess down the cellar stairs, his boots scuffing heavily on the damp grit of the stone risers. Below, the lower cellar smelled entirely of turned wine—sharp, biting vinegar rising like frost off the flagstones.

Clara was already there among the double row of casks, moving frantically from head to head with a glass pipette and a box of reagent strips. Beside cask four, the thirty-litre glass carboy sat in its woven wicker jacket, its pale neck still filmed with the dregs of the wash that had ruined everything.

"The acid levels are off the chart," Clara murmured, not looking up as their shadows stretched across the floor. She dipped a strip into a fresh draw, her hands trembling so hard the paper slapped against the rim of her sampling vial. "Every single one. How could a cellar contamination spread this fast? It makes no sense. The pressings were clean when they came off the slats."

Bruno stepped past the tasting table, stopping two paces short of his sister. He did not look at her. Instead, he pulled two slips of paper from his breast pocket and held them out toward Tess—a cashier's voucher for twelve hundred dollars, endorsed on the back, and a typed recommendation on estate stationery granting her an unconditional discharge.

"Take it," Bruno said, his voice flat, pitch-dry. "You've earned your severance. Get your coat and clear out of the valley."

Clara's head snapped up, her face pale under the bare hanging bulbs. "You're paying her out? Bruno, twelve barrels are soured! We have nothing to bottle, nothing to sell to the distributor, and you're handing out wage releases?"

"The vintage was dead in November, Clara," Bruno said coldly, turning half an eye toward her. "The hail took half the hillside before we ever picked a cluster. The notes at the bank don't stop accruing interest because ice hit the valley. You were going to lose the place by spring anyway. Why drag an itinerant hand down into the mud with us?"

He spoke as if reciting a ledger entry, utterly detached. In his pocket, the matter was closed.

Tess took the voucher and the letter, sliding them into the inner breast pocket of her work jacket where she could feel the stiff paper against her ribs. But her fingers brushed the folded sheaf of the buyout agreement still tucked beneath her wool lining. The Highline Crest Resorts contract. Section four laid it out in plain, unsparing prose: full debt assumption and cash settlement for the Callow estate, contingent upon the immediate, permanent cessation of all commercial viticulture.

She could walk away. The money was in her pocket. Bruno had kept his word, buying her compliance with twelve hundred dollars that would take her to Oregon or the coast, far clear of the Callow family's ruin. If she walked up the stairs now, Bruno would weather Clara's anger, blame the loss on poor sanitation or cellar incompetence, and quietly let the bank foreclose into the developer's waiting hands.

Bruno glanced at her, a warning flicker in his gray eyes: *We had a deal.*

Tess looked at Clara, whose sleeves were soaked red to the forearms, testing cask after cask as though sheer frantic labor could pull the vinegar back out of the wood. The cruelty of it sat between them, ugly and deliberate. Bruno hadn't blundered. He had taken Clara's unpasteurized dessert pressings and deliberately dosed the topping carboy with live vinegar wash to force the collapse.

Tess took a slow step backward toward the tasting table. Keeping silent wasn't survival anymore; it was an endorsement. She slipped her right hand inside her jacket, withdrew the thick folded contract, and set it squarely face-up on the zinc tasting table, right beside Clara's rows of litmus strips and sample vials.

"What is that?" Clara asked, her voice hitching as she wiped her stained fingers against her apron and stepped toward the table.

Bruno turned, saw the crested letterhead, and lunged. "Clara, don't touch that—"

Clara was faster. She snatched the stiff parchment off the metal, unfolding it with a snap that cracked in the vaulted room. Her eyes tore down the first page, skipping the legal preamble straight to the liability clause, to Bruno's signature, and to the rider stipulating that winemaking operations must cease before the deed could transfer.

"Highline Crest?" Clara's whisper seemed to drain the remaining warmth from the cellar. She looked from the paper to the wicker-clad carboy, then slowly up to her brother. "You didn't stretch the volume because you were reckless. You poured the sour mother into the carboy on purpose."

Bruno stood frozen under the glare of the work light, his jaw tight, his hands clenching into fists at his sides. He offered no denial. He offered nothing at all.

"You poisoned our wine," Clara said, the words barely carrying across the damp stone, hollowed out and ruined. "You poisoned my harvest to break the estate."

Tess stepped past them without a word. She didn't check to see if Bruno would try to stop her; his eyes were fixed on his sister, trapped in the wreckage he had bought. She reached the bottom step, her boots biting into the grit, and started up into the gray mountain light, the draft slip secure against her chest, leaving the Callow heirs standing together in the sour quiet of their ruined vintage.
