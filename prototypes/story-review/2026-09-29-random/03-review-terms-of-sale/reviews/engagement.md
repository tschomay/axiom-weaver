# Engagement and Believability — Terms of Sale

**Score: 2/5** — The opening pressure and the match-burning temptation hold attention, but the story's central reversal (Jean was honest all along) contradicts what the reader watched her do, and the romance and the resolution rest on turns that are asserted rather than earned.

## Findings

### 1. [major] Jean's vindication contradicts her own on-page concealment
- **Where:** Scene 3, then Scene 7 and Scene 9
- **Quote:** Scene 3: "The cellar was the very last precinct on the English coastline she could permit this perceptive man to inspect." / Scene 7: "I never lied to you about the foundation, Nicholas!" / Scene 9: "the woman who had warned him about the southern bay window when silence would have cleared her debt."
- **Problem:** The reader watched Jean lie about the sash ("Just the coastal damp"), invent a dead breaker to keep him out of the cellar, and steer him away "praying he would look at the horizon." She never warned him about anything. Scene 9 then asks both Nicholas and the reader to accept that she was honest throughout, and the story's whole moral payoff hangs on that. A reader stops believing: Nicholas's suspicion was correct, and the "reveal" does not answer it.
- **Reader needed:** Either an on-page moment where Jean discloses (or was about to) before she's caught, or a story that admits she was tempted and chose honesty only after being caught.

### 2. [major] The waiver "proves" her innocence while describing the opposite
- **Where:** Scene 5, Scene 9
- **Quote:** Scene 9: "*Gallagher & Sons declines to market without mandatory disclosure.*" / "hiding it would have let her pocket a buyer's deposit and walked away clean." / Scene 5: "Without the waiver staring a solicitor in the face, the sale might slide through on momentum alone."
- **Problem:** The clause says the firm won't market without disclosure, yet Jean marketed it, showed it to a cash buyer, and locked the waiver in a drawer where no one would see it. Not burning a paper isn't the same as disclosing it. So the key-and-drawer reveal, which is meant to shame Nicholas, actually supports his charge. Jean's "You could have handed this to Brenda..." / "And let her dump the liability" exchange doesn't explain what she was protecting.
- **Reader needed:** A clear reason why keeping the waiver in the file counted as disclosure, e.g. it was already in the pack going to his solicitors.

### 3. [major] Jean's sacrifice costs her nothing she hadn't already lost
- **Where:** Scene 7 to Scene 8
- **Quote:** Scene 7: "I am legally bound by a whistleblower mandate to file a statutory condemnation notice... It will be published in the registry by noon tomorrow." / Scene 8: "Yet the suffocating weight... did not crush her... feeling ruined, bankrupt, and entirely, utterly free."
- **Problem:** Jean withdraws the listing only after the buyer has exposed the subsidence and promised to condemn the house publicly, so the sale was already dead. Her "choice" in Scene 8 is taking a loss she couldn't avoid, but the prose treats it as a noble climax. The one real choice she makes (refusing to burn the waiver, Scene 5) is low-stakes, because she keeps it hidden anyway.
- **Reader needed:** A moment where honesty costs Jean something she could still have kept, with the sale still possible, so that the climax is her doing.

### 4. [major] The courtship is asserted rather than built
- **Where:** Scene 3, Scene 6, Scene 10
- **Quote:** Scene 6: "For nearly an hour they had wandered through the cavernous upper suites, speaking not of cubic yards or asking prices, but of the strange, solitary geometry of their working lives." / Scene 10: "You threw away twelve years of standing to keep my name out of the Gazette?"
- **Problem:** The premise is that she "falls for" him, but the only warm exchange is one balcony conversation, and the hour that bonds them is summarised in a sentence. Nicholas spends Scenes 4, 7 and 9 despising her. Then in Scene 10, a few hours after calling her a would-be killer, he wrecks his career for her and proposes a partnership. The change of heart has no scene of its own. It happens between Scene 9 and Scene 10.
- **Reader needed:** At least one dramatised exchange where each sees something in the other that justifies the leap, and a beat showing Nicholas deciding to resign.

### 5. [minor] Scene 9 re-reveals what the reader has already seen twice
- **Where:** Scene 9 (after Scene 5 and Scene 8)
- **Quote:** "The combination is nineteen forty-eight. The year my grandfather hung the shingle. Spin it yourself."
- **Problem:** The reader has watched the waiver locked away (Scene 5) and produced for Brenda (Scene 8), with the same dial combination both times. Scene 9 plays the drawer-opening as a suspense beat, but the only suspense left is Nicholas's reaction, and it's drawn out across the key toss, the dial and the unfolding. Attention slips.
- **Reader needed:** New information or a shorter beat. Scene 9 should turn on something the reader doesn't already know.

### 6. [minor] The ticking clocks are set up and then dropped
- **Where:** Scene 1, Scene 7, Scene 8, Scene 9
- **Quote:** Scene 1: "Thirty days from today, or the lease on these premises reverts to Keel Holdings" / Scene 7: "we're cut off until midnight." / Scene 8: "Gallagher and Sons belongs to the Keel Trust as of sunset."
- **Problem:** The thirty-day deadline shrinks to the same day without a real trigger (Brenda simply has a warrant ready). The sluice trap, built up from Scene 1 as the big afternoon threat, lasts no time at all, because Jean is ferried back and in the office an hour later. Neither pressure device pays off, so the build-up feels wasted.
- **Reader needed:** Deadlines that actually constrain what the characters can do, or a stated reason why they collapse early.

### 7. [minor] Nicholas's resignation is a legal gimmick with unclear benefit
- **Where:** Scene 10
- **Quote:** "Except the mandate applies only to accredited inspectors of the Trust. As of six o'clock, I am merely an unemployed gentleman with an expensive pair of calipers and an unfiled draft in his pocket."
- **Problem:** The agency is already repossessed and the listing already withdrawn, so it's unclear what his notice would now do to Jean. A dangerous house also stays unflagged, which sits oddly with his Scene 7 outrage ("A family could have been sleeping above that bay"). The grand gesture feels arranged for the ending.
- **Reader needed:** A concrete harm to Jean that only his resignation prevents, and an acknowledgement of what happens to the house.

### 8. [nit] The undercover premise and the eavesdropping rely on convenience
- **Where:** Scene 2, Scene 4
- **Quote:** Scene 4: "If The Redoubt doesn't close—if this London gentleman doesn't sign on the line by Friday—Gallagher and Sons is finished."
- **Problem:** The agency's only clerk airs its finances loudly in the pub where the undercover buyer happens to be sitting. That, plus a trust that hires a surveyor to pose as a buyer when an ordinary survey would do, makes the setup feel convenient.
- **Reader needed:** A reason the information reaches Nicholas that doesn't depend on Toby being careless in public.

## What works
- Scene 5's temptation is concrete and tense, and it's the one moment where Jean's integrity is tested on the page: "It's three seconds with a Lucifer, Jean."
- Brenda is vivid and specific from her first appearance: "Brenda Keel did not remove her pigskin gloves when indoors. To remove them would suggest a willingness to linger, or worse, sympathy."
- The shared tally in Scene 10 gives the ending a playful rhythm specific to these two: "A catastrophic balance sheet," / "Disastrous," Nicholas agreed.

## Out of lane
Jean's "telephone" with a "glowing display" (Scene 6) clashes with the gaslamps, gigs and vesta cases of this world. That's for the consistency reviewer.
