The basement porter locker room of St. Jude’s had the damp, subterranean dignity of a root cellar that had long forgotten what turnips smelled like, subsisting instead on pine oil, wet wool, and the faint, persistent sulfur of subterranean boiler pipes. Outside, the November rain was having a violent personal grievance with the pavement, pelting the high clerestory gratings with the rhythmic fury of an irate scullery maid rattling her colanders. Down below, Julian Miller cared very little about the weather’s hysterics, provided the ceiling held.

He had thirty-six minutes before muster—time enough to unpack his kit, shake out his stiff wool coat, and ensure his relocation savings envelope was still nestled securely in his breast pocket. It was. The envelope was creased at the corners like a bakery bag of sugar, plump with five-pound notes that were supposed to match Clara’s deposit on the joinery shop in Hull before January threw its bitter freezing fit. If Julian could survive eight weeks of winter shifts, bad tea, and Supervisor Gable’s hawkish glare, he would be planing sweet cedar shavings two hundred miles north of this masonry pit.

Locker B-14 had been allotted to him that morning with a brass key that looked like it had been chewed on by an apologetic dog. Julian slotted the key into the heavy, stiff padlock, giving it the sharp upward wiggle required of institutional ironmongery. The hasp sprang free with a shriek. But as Julian reached to unhook it, his knuckle caught against the bottom row of louvered vents stamped into the olive-drab sheet metal.

A sharp clack echoed across the empty bench rows. The lowest horizontal metal slat had simply dropped half an inch on its left flank, revealing itself to be entirely unbolted—a loose metal baffle that someone had nudged askew, leaving a neat, two-finger aperture into the locker’s innards without ever disturbing the padlock above.

Julian frowned, crouching down. Institutional lockers were supposed to be petty fortresses, yet here was an aperture wide enough to post a loaf of bread through by installments. Peering through the gap into the shadowy cavity beneath the locker’s bottom shelf, his fingers met cold metal rather than dust bunnies.

He pulled it out: a blue tin cigar box, decorated with chipped gilt lithography of Caribbean palm fronds and faint scratches where fingernails had prised at the lid. Inside was no illicit tobacco. Resting on the painted tin bottom was a folded leaf of cheap hospital requisition paper, inked in a looping, shockingly florid hand that belonged either to an Edwardian poetaster or a medical student with far too much time on his hands.

*"To whoever shares this sovereign tin: the night shift is an exile of damp corridors, yet hope flickers like tallow. Let us not be ghosts to each other."*

Julian blinked at the theatrical scribble. He shook his head, amused despite himself, tucked the parchment back beneath the palm fronds, and slid the tin under the shelf again, his thoughts already returning to the quiet timber of Hull and the cash notes warming against his ribs.

Supervisor Irene Gable did not believe in transitional lulls. To her mind, the five minutes between the ending of one hospital shift and the beginning of another were a moral hazard through which indolence, theft, and unsanctioned fraternization crept like damp through floorboards. She stood at the lip of the Sub-Basement Loading Bay—a cavernous, concrete trough smelling of diesel exhaust, institutional cabbage, and damp gravel—clutching a zinc clipboard with the ferocious grip of a person who suspected every document was trying to stage an escape.

Outside, the evening sleet was throwing a petulant tantrum against the bay’s corrugated iron roll-top doors, hammering them with the petty vindictiveness of a disappointed landlord. Gable ignored it. Her small, sharp eyes swept over the half-dozen day porters shivering in their stained twill coats, lingering by the salt bins as though hoping to turn into statues before anyone asked them to haul laundry.

"Straighten up, the lot of you," Gable barked, her voice slicing through the thrum of the idling coal lorries. "Look at you—standing around like a stack of chipped teacups on a greasy draining board, waiting for an accident to happen! Miller! Put your thumbs in your pockets on your own time."

Julian started, hastily dropping his hands to his sides. His coat collar was turned up against the draft, though Gable could see the telltale signs of a man whose wits were three miles elsewhere—probably scheming about that joinery shop up north he never quite ceased muttering about.

"Listen to me very carefully," Gable continued, pacing the damp salt-dusted apron with the brisk, twitchy precision of an elderly hen who suspected the worms were plotting treason. She tapped the edge of the zinc clipboard against her knuckles—*clack, clack, clack*—until the porters shuffled into an apologetic line. "Winter handover regulations began at midnight. Dr. Thorpe and the administrative committee have made their displeasure abundantly plain regarding off-shift loitering. The six o’clock lockout is absolute."

"Supervisor," began old Craddock from the linen desk, clearing his throat, "the east service lift has been stuck on three since tea, and if the night lads don't get the hampers—"

"The night lads have their own hands, Craddock, and if they lack them, they may petition the parish!" Gable snapped, cutting him off mid-drawl. "Let me be entirely transparent so none of you may plead mutton-headed ignorance tomorrow morning. If any day porter is caught on hospital property three minutes past the six o’clock shift handover—whether skulking in the tunnels, cadging tea in the boiler cellar, or visiting their private lockers—they will be written up for immediate disciplinary suspension. Not a stern talking-to. Not a docked half-crown. A suspension, followed by outright dismissal if the board confirms it."

She leveled her glare directly at Julian, whose chin had retreated slightly into his muffler. 

"You work your hours," Gable said, her gaze pinning him like a specimen on cork, "you clear your gear, and you vacate the premises before the evening bells stop echoing. The hospital does not pay for your presence when your badge is off the board. Am I understood?"

"Understood, Supervisor," Julian said, his voice flat, steady, and entirely weary.

"Right then," Gable said, making an aggressive checkmark against her roster sheet that nearly sheared the paper in two. "Day roster dismissed. Clear the docks before the night vans back in."

Leaning against the radiator pipes was Clara. She was bundled into an oversized tweed coat, tapping one scarred leather boot with the rhythmic, predatory patience of a kestrel eyeing a vole in an open furrow. As soon as Julian rounded the corner, her chin came up.

"You're five minutes late off the bays," she said, not bothering to trade pleasantries. "And Mr. Garside called from the docks this morning. He says if the second installment isn't in his ledger before the stroke of midnight on the thirty-first of January, our joinery shop goes back on the open market. "He holds the keys, Julian! Which makes his rattling entirely lethal." Clara stepped closer, her breath smoking faintly in the unheated stairwell, crossing her arms like a barricade. "He has three cabinetmakers from Leeds offering cash over the barrel. I paid my three hundred quid from the workshop sale; you're the anchor dragging the boat. Julian reached into the inner breast pocket of his wool jacket, his fingers brushing the thick, reassuring bulk of the relocation envelope. It sat nestled against his ribs, dense and slightly dog-eared, packed with the crumpled twenty-pound notes and wage-packets he had hoarded through endless, spine-snapping shifts. He had accumulated nearly the full deposit sum inside the Hull lease deposit envelope—only a single week's pay short of the final target.

He patted the coat fabric with a firm, hollow thud. "I have almost all of it. Ninety-five pounds left to square away, Clara, and Gable pays out Friday. You'll have every last pence before January turns. I give you my word."

Clara stared at his coat for a long beat, as though calculating the exact density of paper against wool, before exhaling a breath that ruffled her woollen scarf. "You'd better. Because if we lose that shop on the Humber over ninety-five miserable pounds, I won't just kill you, Julian—I'll make you build your own pine box first."

Julian snapped the cigar box shut, tucked it deep beneath the shelf, and swung the locker door closed until the latch caught. Slipping his brass padlock into place, he stepped out into the corridor just as the six o'clock siren began its wail.

And yet—because cowardice had its limits, and the cedar wren in his pocket burned against his thigh like a living coal—he added that every word of admiration he had offered in return had been his own, hopelessly sincere and thoroughly undeserved.

"Miller."

The voice arrived without the courtesy of footsteps, landing with the flat, greasy thud of a wet flour sack dropped onto an unscrubbed scullery table.

Julian jumped, his shoulders hunching toward his ears with the guilty startle of a barn owl surprised mid-theft in a pigeon loft. Supervisor Irene Gable stood square in the doorway, blocking the corridor light. Her metal clipboard was clamped beneath her left arm like a cudgel, her heavy storm coat unbuttoned, and her nostrils flared with the cold, predatory satisfaction of a hound that had tracked its quarry directly to an open cupboard.

"Ma'am," Julian choked out, instinctively stepping back until his hips hit the rim of the desk. "I was—there was a late requisition slip for the north ward—"

"The north ward requisitions cleared at four-fifteen," Gable said, her tone dropping into that flat, rehearsed cadence supervisors reserve for delivering administrative executions. She stepped across the threshold, tapping the brass clip of her board against her hip with dry, rhythmic cruelty. "The night porters clocked their cards twenty minutes ago. Your card, Miller, was stamped out at six sharp. Yet here you sit, skulking around the dispatch corridor well after eight o'clock in flagrant, deliberate contempt of the lockout protocol."

"I only lost track of the hour—"

"You lost track of your employment, which is rather more serious," Gable cut in, her small, sharp eyes pinning him against the baize board. She did not glance at his haversack, but her gaze was sharp enough to bore through brass. "Hospital management does not heat these halls so off-shift porters can wander about like vagrants seeking shelter. Lockout is absolute. You were warned at shift handover, and you chose to treat the regulation as a polite suggestion."

Julian opened his mouth, but every prepared lie—a mislaid key, a sudden wave of vertigo, an errand for dispatch—evaporated before the rigid line of her jaw.

"You are placed on disciplinary suspension, effective immediately," Gable stated, scrawling a sharp, tearing stroke across a duplicate voucher on her clipboard before tearing it free. "Pending formal administrative review on Monday morning. Leave your brass security token on the hook. If I catch you past the service gate before the committee summons you, you will be dismissed on the spot without references. Do you understand me, Julian?"

"Yes, ma'am," he whispered, the cold air rushing into his lungs as if he had been struck.

Gable pivoted barely an inch to let him by, forcing him to brush past the stiff wool of her coat as he dragged his haversack over his shoulder. He had saved the cedar wren and the tin box from the cellar jaws, but the corridor stretching before him was deserted and bare—no cultivated correspondent, no gentle voice, only the bleak glare of the strip lights and the ruinous certainty that in chasing an echo, he had put Clara’s future on the block.

## Scene 12
<!-- card: scene_12_the_boiler_encounter -->

To be suspended from duty was one thing—a formal, clipboard-bound indignity involving stamped carbon slips and icy stares—but to actually leave the building when the only answers in the world were down in the damp dark was quite another. Julian did not leave. Instead, he slipped downward, following the clank of iron through the subterranean arteries until he reached the boiler substation, that sulfurous brick cavern of hissing expansion joints and caustic solvent sumps where the air always smelled faintly of scalded cabbage.

Upstairs, the rain was evidently having a furious, childish tantrum against the storm drains, rattling the subterranean grating with all the vindictive spite of a wet gutter-cat batting at a cornered beetle. Down here, however, it was hot enough to melt grease. Julian folded himself behind a lagging-wrapped pipe elbow, heart hammering against his ribs, watching the shadows stretch across the concrete. Two o'clock had struck minutes ago—the scheduled chemical solvent run, solitary and despised, the single shift-hour when whoever tended the low-pressure lines was obliged to haul the heavy galvanized drums across the alcove floor without an escort.

Presently, the dull iron skitter of a drum rim scraping along the floor gave way to silence. Julian peered past the valve wheel, half-expecting to see some austere, silver-templed veteran—the cultivated scholar-mechanic his imagination had so lovingly assembled from ink-stained margins, an elder craftsman who knew the grain of walnut and the ache of solitude.

Instead, there was only Neil Hallowell.

Julian blinked, catching his breath behind the asbestos sleeve. Young Hallowell, the night porter—a lad whose uniform was perpetually three sizes too broad in the shoulders, who possessed neither certifications nor seniority, and whose official hospital standing ranked just below the basement mice—was sitting cross-legged on an upturned grease bucket between two shuddering copper risers. Spread across his knees was a flutter of blue-lined paper, smoothed flat with raw, oil-blackened thumbs, and nestled right beside them on a stained rag sat the hand-carved cedar wren.

It was an astonishing picture of domesticity in an industrial dungeon, laid out like a tea tray piled high with chipped saucers and burnt toast crumbs. Julian stepped forward, a boot sole squeaking upon the slick pavers.

Neil’s head jerked up, eyes widening in sheer, unvarnished panic. He made a futile, frantic motion to cover the papers with his forearms, but the tiny carved bird tipped on its breast, rolling against his knee. It was unmistakably Neil who had taken Gary’s abandoned locker-slot and kept answering every folded sheet in that urgent, secret hand, concealing his lowly porter’s badge behind borrowed phrases just to keep the conversation alive.

"You," Julian said, the syllable dropping like lead between them. The refined, poetic gentleman of the furnace rooms dissolved into thin air, leaving only a grease-streaked boy and a fistful of smuggled letters.

## Scene 13
<!-- card: scene_13_the_prank_revealed -->

Neil did not run, though his boots gave a twitch that suggested his heels had strongly considered the option. Instead, he retreated backward out of the heat and clatter of the boilers, clutching the crumpled paper to his chest like an orphan hoarding stolen lard, while Julian followed him in hollow, rigid disbelief out onto the sub-basement loading bay—that echoing trench where exhaust fumes and diesel damp always pooled beneath the iron roll-up shutters.

"Say something," Julian demanded, his voice thin against the hollow concrete. "Don't just stand there gaping at me like a cornered vole. You wrote them? All of them? The lines about the tallow candles, the cedar dust, the—"

"Julian, please, just let me—"

Whatever defense Neil had gathered in his throat was obliterated by the screech of rubber casters bouncing violently over an expansion joint. Gary Vance barged through the plastic strip curtain pushing a pair of overflowing canvas hampers—Gary, that lanky, perpetually grinning former porter whose idea of workplace decorum was generally on par with a stray hound turning over a rubbish pail. He wiped damp suds from his forearms onto his apron, took in the tableau of Julian’s stricken face and Neil’s bunched fists, and promptly let out a sharp, cackling bark of laughter.

"Oh, bloody hell," Gary crowed, leaning all his weight on the laundry cart until the metal frame groaned like an overfed dairy cow being shoved into a milking stall. "Don't tell me he actually found you out! Neil, you didn't! In the locker?"

Julian’s head jerked toward him. "What are you talking about?"

"The note, mate! Locker fourteen!" Gary beamed, entirely blind to the sheer misery vibrating between them. "I left that purple, flowery nonsense jammed behind the mesh six weeks ago before I transferred up to linen—just to see which poor day-shift sod would take the bait and write back to a phantom! Pure theater, honestly. I swore whoever took it would realize it was a lark by teatime!"

A draft slipped under the loading bay door—November air with all the spiteful petulance of a cold tea-towel snapped across bare shins—and settled into Julian's ribcage. His breath caught, sharp and sour. A lark. An old shift prank left behind like a greasy pie crust, casually tossed into a dark locker for laughs.

"You didn't write the first one," Julian whispered, turning back to Neil. The words felt chipped and brittle in his mouth. "It was Gary's joke. And you... you found it?"

Neil’s pale face flushed a violent, humiliating crimson. He kept his eyes fixed firmly on the oil-stained concrete. He had concealed his identity as a mere uncredentialed night porter, sustaining the romantic correspondence that Gary had started in jest, week after week, answering every tender, aching line Julian had pushed beneath the loose louver.

"I found it," Neil admitted, his voice barely louder than the hiss of a distant radiator. "Gary left it. But after you answered... Julian, after I read what you wrote, I couldn't stop. I couldn't bear to let it die. I wrote this for you tonight—before Gable came with the shears. Take it. Please."

With shaking fingers, Neil extended the folded sheet—his unsent confession letter, inked in plain, humble strokes, laying out every quiet truth he had withheld. 

Julian looked down at the creased paper, but he could not bring himself to touch it. The poetry, the solitary craftsman sharing his midnight sorrows, the delicate intimacy he had guarded through double shifts and suspension warnings—it had all begun as a scullery farce, a kitchen maid's pantomime concocted by a bored laundryman. A profound, sick betrayal washed through him, cold and heavy as sodden dough, and for all Neil's frantic apologies, Julian could only stand beneath the yellow dock lamps, entirely undone.

## Scene 14
<!-- card: scene_14_the_lease_choice -->

The dispatch office smelled reliably of stale chicory coffee and dried shipping paste, a combination that had never once in its institutional life inspired anyone to dream of greater things. Behind the counter, standing with both boot heels dug into the linoleum like a stubborn pony refusing the bridle, stood Clara Miller—Julian’s elder sister by fourteen tempestuous months, possessing their late father's hawkish nose, a relentless habit of tapping her thumb against her knuckle when irritated, and very little patience for the contemplative arts. She was shaking the damp mist from her woollen scarf with brisk, violent snaps.

"You are twenty minutes late, Julian, and don't give me that look—the five-forty tram from the docks was on time because I saw it rattle past the bakery," Clara announced without looking up, unbuttoning her mackintosh with the fierce efficiency of a quartermaster. On the green blotter between them, she slapped down a thick packet of parchment tied with butcher’s twine. "The countersigned agreement for the workshop on Prince Street. The landlord gave us until midnight tomorrow to wire the balance, which means your half needs to be in the postal order office before it shuts at seven. Where is the envelope?"

Julian stepped across the threshold, shutting the frost-rimed door behind him. In the corner of the room, sitting beside his damp wool cap, rested the battered blue tin cigar box salvaged from the locker room, its scratched painted lid still carrying the faint, ghostly scent of tobacco and cedar shavings. His coat pocket felt heavy—weighted down by the thick relocation envelope containing months of scrimped wages, missing meals, and scraped-together overtime pence. Every coin had been saved to escape this grim borough and buy cedar stock for Clara’s joinery.

"Julian?" Clara demanded, her sharp gaze fixing upon his face. Outside the clouded transom, the November wind was throwing a petulant tantrum against the guttering, whistling through the cracked drainpipe with the sour pique of a cook discovering sour milk in the morning custard. "You look like you've been fished out of the canal with a gaff. Did Gable catch you? Tell me you didn't let that pompous clerk fine your wages on the very week we're meant to clear out."

"No," Julian said quietly, his voice catching in his throat. "Gable didn't fine me."

"Then produce the money and stop hovering like an errant schoolboy," she snapped, though her tone softened around the edges with genuine, frayed exasperation. She tapped the lease document. "Two benches, Julian. A dry floor with actual south-facing skylights, and no dock bells tolling your life away in fifteen-minute increments. We pack your tools on Saturday, rent the dray, and we're across the river by dawn. Just give me the envelope so I can log the deposit."

Julian looked at the parchment. For six months, those skylights and the smell of clean sawdust had been his solitary north star—the promised reward that had made the freezing shifts and Gable’s spiteful clipboard bearable. It was safety. It was clean, neat, and completely unburdened by midnight whispers on lined scrap paper.

And it was, he realized with a sharp, hollowing certainty, a total retreat.

If he boarded that dray on Saturday, he would spend the rest of his life wondering whether Neil’s tears on the loading bay were real, whether those aching passages about solitary makers had been born of a boy’s quiet desperation or a cheap laugh, and whether he had fled simply because love had arrived wearing an ugly, mortifying disguise. One did not mend a splintered joint by boarding it over with fresh veneer; it had to be planed down to the raw grain, no matter how much sawdust got into your eyes.

"I can't give it to you, Clara," Julian said, his fingers curling tight against the wool inside his pockets.

Clara’s hand froze mid-air above the butcher's twine. She blinked, her chin jerking upward. "What do you mean, you can't? Julian, the deposit is due. We signed the option."

"I can't go to Hull," he said, the words falling between them with the heavy, unceremonious clatter of a pewter kettle dropped onto flagstones. "Not on Saturday. Not like this."

"Have you completely lost your senses?" Clara’s voice climbed an octave, her sharp eyes darting across his pale features in search of a fever or a bad joke. "We planned this for two years! We have the benches spoken for! You’ve spent half your life moaning about the coal dust in this basement, and now you’re standing there telling me—for what? What could possibly keep you in this wretched cellar?"

"Something I have to finish," Julian whispered, though in his chest, the sick knot of betrayal had begun to give way to something far more dangerous: the urgent, terrifying need to look Neil in the eye and find the absolute truth.

## Scene 15
<!-- card: scene_15_neils_departure -->

There is a particular, melancholy dampness that belongs exclusively to hospital sub-basements at four in the morning—a clammy stillness that settles over concrete like congealed gravy left behind in a cracked roasting tin. The basement porter locker room, smelling as it always did of harsh carbolic soap, stale floor wax, and damp coal dust, offered no comforting illusions to anyone foolish enough to seek them. Certainly not to Neil Hallowell, who crept past the silent rows of lockers with the gingerly, apologetic tread of a guilty hound sneaking past the butcher's open door.

Outside, the November gale had evidently worked itself into a righteous, shivering indignation, wheezing through the high ground-level grates with all the sour petulance of a magistrate whose tea had gone stone cold. Neil barely heard it. His eyes were fixed on locker B-14, with its chipped olive paint and that unbolted bottom louver Julian had nudged open so many breathless evenings ago. The locker stood locked and hollow under Gable’s padlock, yet inside—resting just behind that loosened slat where Julian had so nimbly slipped the blue tin cigar box back into hiding before Gable's inspection broom could sweep it away—lay the small, silent metal vessel of their shared madness.

Neil pulled the little wooden bird from his coat pocket. The cedar wren, smoothed and feathered by Julian’s devotion and countless hours of patient whittling, felt impossibly heavy in his palm, smelling sharply of sweet timber and unearned grace. How completely he had ruined it. Gary’s ridiculous, preening prank had given the correspondence its absurd birth, but it was Neil—lonely, cowardly Neil—who had greedily breathed life into the fire, pretending to be a scholar and an equal while Julian poured his honest heart onto paper. Even now, glancing toward the peg where Julian’s work jacket had hung—the one whose inside pocket had carried that thick relocation savings envelope earmarked for timber yards and an escape to Hull—Neil knew that Julian’s suspension was only the beginning of Gable’s vengeance unless someone shouldered the blame.

Carefully, as though touching an unexploded shell, Neil worked his fingers beneath the bottom louver of locker B-14, slid the blue tin lid open by touch, and laid the cedar wren to rest inside. It belonged to the box, and to Julian’s solitary dreams, not to a fraudulent night porter who transported chemical solvent drums in the dark.

From his breast pocket, Neil withdrew a single sheet of lined paper, creased four times into a crisp, neat rectangle: his official resignation letter. In flat, uncompromising strokes, he had penned a full confession, claiming every transgression—the unlocked vent, the contraband box, the breach of shift protocols—as his sole responsibility, ensuring Gable’s spiteful wrath would terminate on him alone and dissolve Julian’s suspension before morning handover. He set the folded resignation on the worn pine bench directly before locker B-14, smoothing the corners flat with his thumb, and turned toward the stairs without looking back.

## Scene 16
<!-- card: scene_16_a_real_beginning -->

Julian did not wait for Gable’s morning inspection, nor did he allow the neatly folded sheet of lined paper to linger upon the pine bench where Neil had so self-sacrificingly deposited it. The human impulse toward martyrdom is, after all, a remarkably tedious spectacle—particularly when accompanied by the dramatic rustle of institutional foolscap—and Julian had entirely run out of patience for theatrical gestures carried out in cold basements.

He caught Neil by the waste dock at the far edge of the loading bay, just as the boy was attempting to hitch his collar against a morning wind that had arrived in a thoroughly vile mood—tossing spiteful spits of sleet against the corrugated awning like an irritable scullery-maid hurling cold dishwater at a stray tomcat. Neil turned, startled, his knuckles raw and his shoulder bags slung across his chest with the desperate, guilty slump of a thieving schoolboy caught mid-scramble over the orchard wall.

"You dropped your dramatics," Julian said, holding up the four-creased rectangle of Neil's official resignation letter.

"Julian—don't. Gable’s coming down at seven," Neil blurted, making a frantic, abortive grab that caught nothing but wet air. "If he finds you here while you're under suspension—"

"He can suspend me until the plumbing rots," Julian cut in smoothly, took the resignation slip between two steady thumbs, and tore it cleanly in half.

Neil made a small, strangled squeak—a sound not unlike a plump hedgehog being unexpectedly hoisted by its bristles—and stared down at the severed pieces as if Julian had just dismantled the Crown Jewels. "What did you do that for? If I take the blame for the locker, for Vance's damned joke, for... for all of it—Gable drops the inquiry. You get your shift back. You go to Hull!"

"I am not going to Hull," Julian said.

Neil blinked, damp curls sticking to his forehead. "You have to. The workshop. Clara took the envelope—"

"Clara took an envelope stuffed with yesterday's manifest duplicates," Julian confessed, offering a wry, crooked twitch of his mouth that felt surprisingly light after the heavy misery of the previous afternoon. "The actual deposit envelope is sitting under my mattress at Mrs. Finch’s, and the lease expired at midnight. I forfeited it, Neil. I let the carpentry shop go."

"You let it go?" Neil's voice climbed half an octave, horrified and bewildered in equal measure. "Because of—because I lied to you? Julian, Vance wrote that first note as a joke! And I kept it up because I was terrified you'd see I was only a greasy night porter who couldn't tell cedar from cheap pine! I deceived you! You were sick with it yesterday!"

"I was," Julian admitted, stepping closer until the smell of the damp river-sleet was edged out by the familiar tang of machine oil and cedar shavings clinging to Neil's coat. The sharp sting of betrayal—that heavy, sour ache of having poured his heart into a prank—had not vanished into thin air, but standing under the raw morning sky, it had shriveled from a tragedy into something distinctly absurd. "I felt like an idiot. I felt mocked. But Vance didn't write the note about the rain on the skylights, did he? Vance didn't write that quiet sentence about how the dark feels lighter when you know someone else is awake in the same brick cage."

Neil swallowed hard, his throat bobbing. "No. I wrote that."

"And you didn't invent that hand-carved wren," Julian added gently. "Even if you tried to leave it behind like a proper martyr. If we are going to do this, Neil—and God knows we probably shouldn't, given the sheer administrative chaos we’ve managed in three weeks—we are not doing it through a loose louver in locker B-14. No pseudonyms. No midnight envelopes tucked into cigar tins. Just you, without the borrowed poetry, and me, without running off to Yorkshire."

Neil looked down at the two halves of his ruined resignation fluttering in Julian's grip, then up into Julian's face, his expression cracking open into something unsteady, fragile, and utterly honest. "I don't know much about poetry, Julian. Not really."

"Splendid," Julian murmured, tossing the scraps toward the waste bin. "Then you can buy me a thoroughly unpoetic cup of tea across the street before Gable wakes up."
