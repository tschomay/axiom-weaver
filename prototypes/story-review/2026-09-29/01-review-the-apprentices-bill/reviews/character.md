# Character Coherence — The Apprentice's Bill

**Score: 3/5** — The load-bearing choice (Julien rescuing the dish of the man who ruined him) has to be guessed from outside, because his interior goes dark from Scene 2 to the end. His Scene 1 vow never says what he means to do, and André moves from thief to merciful man with nothing shown in between.

## Findings

### 1. [major] Julien's turn from vengeance to rescue happens off the page
- **Where:** Scene 2 → Scene 3
- **Quote:** "He had come expecting to look upon an emperor gorging himself on stolen grain. He had not prepared for mercy." (Scene 2); "Instead, Julien set the torch upon the pass and took the copper saucier." (Scene 3)
- **Problem:** Julien's last inner reaction is surprise at André's kindness to the commis. We never see what he makes of the palsy, which is the real revelation. The next time we meet him, he is already saving the dish. The only reason given for the rescue is the negative "not from pride, but from the unyielding memory of the labor itself." That explains how his hands move, not why he chose to help. The story's central choice is left for the reader to infer.
- **Reader needed:** One beat of Julien taking in André's shaking hands and deciding, even a look or a single thought, so the rescue reads as his choice and not the plot's.

### 2. [major] The Scene 1 vow never says what Julien intends to do
- **Where:** Scene 1 (paid off in Scene 3)
- **Quote:** "He would not simply watch from the periphery while André stumbled into an easy excuse; he swore he would see André's name burned off the ticket before the final course ever cleared the pass."
- **Problem:** Julien has just smelled that the smoker is ruining the dish, so André is already failing publicly. Getting up to intervene works against his own revenge unless he has some plan, and the prose never gives one. It is unclear what "an easy excuse" is or why stopping it means walking into the kitchen. The vow only makes sense in hindsight, as a set-up for the literal blowtorch, so it reads as the author's wordplay rather than a goal Julien actually holds.
- **Reader needed:** A clear sense of what Julien means to do when he stands up (expose, sabotage, confront) so the reader can feel it change.

### 3. [minor] André's villainy and his mercy are never connected
- **Where:** Scene 1 and Scene 2
- **Quote:** "then set his whispering campaign through every dining syndicate in the capital until Julien could not even scrub copper pots in a brasserie" (Scene 1); "Andre caught the boy's wrist with surprising softness and stepped between him and the line." (Scene 2)
- **Problem:** The man who stole a recipe and blacklisted an apprentice now takes the blame for a commis's mistake. Surprise is allowed, but nothing links the two: not the illness, not guilt, not age. The reader can't tell whether André has changed, was always two-sided, or is simply whatever each scene needs.
- **Reader needed:** A hint of why this André is gentle, so the contrast deepens him instead of splitting him in two.

### 4. [minor] The reckoning has no exchange between the two men
- **Where:** Scene 3
- **Quote:** "André remained motionless, his shoulders shaking with a slow, devastating gratitude that seemed to hollow out whatever remained of his stature, his lips parted in a silent, pleading apology that found no purchase."
- **Problem:** Across the whole story André speaks once, to Luc, and Julien speaks once, to a runner. At their climax, the narrator names André's gratitude and apology and neither man says a word. Julien's silence can be read as a choice, but André's feelings are asserted rather than shown. Neither voice is ever tested against the other, so the story's central relationship is described rather than played out.
- **Reader needed:** One gesture or line from André that shows the apology, and some sign that Julien heard it and turned it down.

### 5. [minor] Julien's final exit is told, not earned
- **Where:** Scene 3
- **Quote:** "pushed through the heavy iron door into the rain-soaked alley behind the kitchen, leaving the heat and the name behind him forever in the dark."
- **Problem:** "Forever" announces an arc of letting go. Nothing before this shows Julien wanting to let go, or weighing whether to take the credit that is now within reach (the name is burned and the critic has the dish).
- **Reader needed:** A moment where Julien could claim the dish or the credit and visibly chooses not to.

### 6. [nit] The critic is only a name
- **Where:** Scene 1
- **Quote:** "Two banquettes away sat Giles Lambert, the chief critic whose ink-stained ledger had consecrated and ruined dining houses across three provinces, his napkin already tucked"
- **Problem:** Lambert is introduced by full name and reputation, then never acts or reacts. He exists only to raise the stakes.
- **Reader needed:** Either one reaction from him, or no name at all.

## What works
- The commis beat is a strong way to complicate André through action: "Leave it, Luc... The reduction was mine. I left it too thin to hold the spoon."
- Julien's precise, controlled manner stays the same from start to finish, from "folding its creases flat with deliberate, surgical precision" (Scene 1) to "his voice level and dead as cold slate" (Scene 3).
- André's fear in front of Julien is shown physically, not stated: "In the old man's rheumy eyes there was neither fury nor defense, only the curdled dread of a hollowed vessel waiting to be shattered."

## Out of lane
Julien sits at "Table 4" in Scene 1 but calls the critic's plate for "Table nine" in Scene 3 (consistency).
