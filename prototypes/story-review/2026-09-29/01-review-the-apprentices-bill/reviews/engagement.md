# Engagement and Believability — The Apprentice's Bill

**Score: 3/5** — The setup is good and the Scene 2 reveal genuinely turned me around, but the climax only works because a whole kitchen brigade freezes while a diner takes over service, and the ending walks away from the questions the story raised: the critic, the credit, and the reckoning between the two men.

## Findings

### 1. [major] A stranger takes over the pass and nobody stops him
- **Where:** Scene 2 and Scene 3
- **Quote:** "Julien slipped through the service vestibule, leaving the muffled velvet of the dining room" (Scene 2); "When his hand descended over the brass casing of the station blowtorch, the steel line went utterly still." (Scene 3); "A runner dashed forward, lifting the silver cloche and the plate in a single panicked blur"
- **Problem:** A guest in a wool coat walks off the dining floor into a working kitchen on review night. He hides by the cooler, then lights a torch at the pass, burns the ticket, plates the critic's dessert and rings the bell. The expeditor who "inhaled to scream" at a spilled glaze says nothing. The runner simply obeys. The payoff rests on everyone in a busy kitchen going passive, and at that point I stopped believing the scene.
- **Reader needed:** A reason the brigade lets him act: someone recognises him as the man who built the dish, André waves them off, or the chaos gives him cover.

### 2. [major] The vow is kept by a gesture with no consequence
- **Where:** Scene 1 and Scene 3
- **Quote:** "he swore he would see André's name burned off the ticket before the final course ever cleared the pass" (Scene 1); "André Laurent's name vanished in a sharp hiss of blackening ash… The vow was kept." (Scene 3)
- **Problem:** Scene 1 frames the stakes as ruin and theft: a stolen masterwork and a blacklisting so thorough that Julien "could not even scrub copper pots." The vow then turns out to be literal, and burning a slip of paper changes nothing for either man. The critic still gets the dish as André's. "The vow was kept" reads like a pun resolving the plot, not a reckoning.
- **Reader needed:** The burned name should cost André something or win Julien something that matters, such as credit, the critic's knowledge, or a public acknowledgement.

### 3. [major] The critic and the credit never pay off
- **Where:** Scene 1 and Scene 3
- **Quote:** "Giles Lambert, the chief critic whose ink-stained ledger had consecrated and ruined dining houses across three provinces, his napkin already tucked" (Scene 1); "leaving the heat and the name behind him forever in the dark." (Scene 3)
- **Problem:** The premise and Scene 1 load the review as the ticking clock. Then the dish goes out and the story leaves by the back door before anyone tastes it. We never learn whether Julien's rescue saved André's reputation, entrenched the theft, or exposed it. The central question (who gets the bill?) is left open by omission, not by design.
- **Reader needed:** Some sign of what the rescue means for the credit, even one beat of the critic's reaction or Julien knowingly giving the dish away.

### 4. [minor] The turn from revenge to rescue happens off the page
- **Where:** Scene 3
- **Quote:** "Instead, Julien set the torch upon the pass and took the copper saucier."
- **Problem:** Scene 2 sets up the change well: the mercy to Luc, the pills, the tremor, "He had not prepared for mercy." But the moment Julien decides to save the man who ruined him is a single "Instead." He burns the name and then rescues the dish, and we get no sense of why he does both. The emotional climax is the most rushed beat in the story.
- **Reader needed:** One moment of Julien weighing the choice, so the rescue reads as his decision and not a pivot the plot requires.

### 5. [minor] The reckoning has no exchange between the two men
- **Where:** Scene 3
- **Quote:** "Julien did not speak." / "his lips parted in a silent, pleading apology that found no purchase. Julien did not linger to hear it."
- **Problem:** The story is billed as a reckoning between apprentice and mentor, but they never trade a word or a direct look. Silence can be powerful. Here it keeps the one confrontation the story promised from happening, and André stays a passive figure of pity.
- **Reader needed:** At least one direct beat between them (a line, a look held, a refusal) that makes the silence feel chosen.

### 6. [minor] How the blacklisted chef got this table is never addressed
- **Where:** Scene 1
- **Quote:** "André had taken the formulation, claimed it as his masterwork, and then set his whispering campaign through every dining syndicate in the capital"
- **Problem:** A man so thoroughly shut out is seated at L'Hiver on review night, and André apparently doesn't know he's there. The coincidence isn't fatal, but it's noticeable, and it's the premise's hook.
- **Reader needed:** One clause on how Julien got the reservation, or whether he planned the night.

### 7. [nit] The cream fix strains culinary belief
- **Where:** Scene 3
- **Quote:** "The cold bound the separated oils; the glossy curd snapped back into cohesive tautness, holding the hay smoke suspended in velvet suspension."
- **Problem:** The story set up the failure with credible technical detail (pectin, overheating). The cure is instant and nearly magical, and a food-literate reader may balk.
- **Reader needed:** A fix that sounds as hard-won as the failure did.

## What works
- The failing dish is set up in Scene 1 and paid off in Scene 3: "The kitchen's cold-smoking apparatus was running far too hot, Julien knew instantly". Julien's expertise is shown before it matters.
- The Scene 2 turn reframes the villain and earns the shift: "He had come expecting to look upon an emperor gorging himself on stolen grain. He had not prepared for mercy."
- The tremor is concrete and specific to this world: "rattling his wedding band against the metal in a frantic, stuttering drumroll… would shatter fine sugar cages and reduce a quenelle to pulp".

## Out of lane
Julien sits at "Table 4" in Scene 1 but sends the dish to "Table nine" in Scene 3 (consistency).
