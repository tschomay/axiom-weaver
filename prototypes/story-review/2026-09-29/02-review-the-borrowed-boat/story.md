## Scene 1

The brass cylinder was cold enough to bite through wool. Ruth Miller settled Arthur's ash urn into the narrow locker beneath the Shearwater's chart bench, wedging it firm between two rolled storm jibs so it would not rattle against the spruce hull. Her father had kept the boat forty years, and in the end he had shrunk to four pounds of grey clinker and a brass seal that would not take polish.

Beside the locker lay Arthur's oilcloth logbook, curled at the corners from damp salt. Ruth flipped it open against the cabin's swinging gimbal lamp. Behind the smoke-tinted chimney glass, the wick threw a greasy yellow oval across pages of tide tables and crab tallies. In the margin beside an offshore fuel bunkering entry, three lines in her father's tidy copperplate caught her eye. They were domestic mooring coordinates, tucked right where an auxiliary petrol log ought to run, all pointing inland toward the private creeks of Tern Bay. Arthur had never owned land in Tern Bay, not on any deed Ruth had ever seen during three years of scrubbing his linoleum and sorting his pills.

Heavy boot-heels struck the deck above. Helen dropped through the companionway hatch, smelling of train smoke and wet tweed. She had left Skiff Cove Harbour five winters back for a ledger clerk's stool in Inverness, and she still carried herself like a woman who believed salt water could be argued with on paper. She did not look at the urn. She glanced instead at the rot along the deck beams, calculating timber footage and what a buyer down south might pay for thirty feet of seasoned larch.

"The tide is turning, Ruth," Helen said, peeling off her town gloves. "We run her down the sound, see the ashes over the side by Tuesday, and sign her away before the autumn gales take the roof off the wheelhouse. Donald is on the jetty with his book."

Donald Crane stood waiting beneath the rusted crane on the slipway, leaning his bad hip against an iron bollard. He had overseen Skiff Cove since before their mother died, and his temper had narrowed to the thickness of harbour board forms. He thrust a grease-stained sheet of carbon paper into Helen's ribs before she had both feet on the stone.

"Provisional seaworthiness waiver," Donald said. His voice was like shingle grinding under a swell. "Old Arthur didn't keep the bilge pump inspected, and the timber under the pintle is soft as cheese. Seven days, Helen. The clause is printed plain on the red duplicate. You bring the Shearwater back into Skiff Cove within the week or the harbour board impounds the mooring and bills you for salvage."

Helen signed with Donald's stub of pencil, her mouth set in a thin line, and thrust the board back into his coat pocket. She gave Donald neither thanks nor goodbye.

Ruth cast off the bowline, stepped aboard the foredeck, and hauled the dripping hemp over the gunwale. The Shearwater backed into the heavy grey chop of the inshore passage, her diesel coughing once before settling into its low, iron rattle. Behind them, Donald remained motionless by his crane, watching them slide away between the wet teeth of the skerries.

## Scene 2

The Inshore Sound was no true channel, but a drowned trench where two opposing tides met to tear at each other over shelves of shale. The swell rose in greasy heaps, thick and pale as melted tallow. Where the race sheared sideways toward the sunken reef of the Black Bar, the froth unraveled across the rocks like frayed silver fringe on a cast-off shawl.

At the helm, Helen stood braced against the engine casing. The kick of the rudder slammed through the spokes, shivering up her forearms each time a swell caught the deadwood. Her wool coat was already dark with salt. She threw her weight hard to starboard, teeth clenched, cursing into the wind as the current dragged the keel toward the white water boiling along the bar.

"Bear away!" Ruth shouted over the diesel.

"The rudder's taking nothing!" Helen’s voice cracked. "She won't bite the stream!"

Ruth hauled herself forward along the narrow side-deck, one arm hooked over the cabin coaming. The spruce mast groaned in its tabernacle, shuddering under the pitch of each short, vicious sea. Forward at the stemhead, something screeched—a sharp, thin note of bronze in distress, shriller than the wind.

She dropped to her knees on the wet fir planking. At the foot of the groaning spar, the starboard forestay took the brunt of the leeward torque. Ruth wiped brine from the turnbuckle with the heel of her mitten. Along the curve of the tang, the metal bracket bolting wire to timber, a hairline fracture split the bronze like a seam in cooling hearth-slag. Under each heavy roll, the crack opened into a dark mouth no thicker than a fingernail, weeping rust before biting shut again.

A sensible woman would have spun the boat. Skiff Cove lay barely four miles astern; Donald’s slipway had dry timber, an iron anvil, and six days left on the harbour waiver. A sensible woman would have put the helm over before the wire parted and took the mast out of her.

Ruth laid her palm flat against the trembling stay. Beneath her knees, through two inches of oak and bilge water, the engine drummed its stubborn rhythm. In the cuddy below, Arthur’s brass-sealed jar sat wrapped in canvas alongside his grease-stained notebook. If they turned back, Donald would see the flaw, Helen would refuse to clear the bar a second time, and whatever Arthur had hidden at Tern Bay would stay drowned in his arithmetic.

"Is the wire holding?" Helen screamed from the stern, fighting the spokes as the bow finally shouldered through the race.

Ruth wiped her wet hand against her oilskins. "Sound enough," she called back, her voice level. "Keep her head west. Give Morren Head two cables' clearance."

By midday, the roar of the sound fell away into the grey drizzle behind them. The cliffs of Morren Head loomed ahead over the bow, and the cracked bronze fitting held fast against the heave of the open swell.

## Scene 3

The black basalt teeth of Morren Reach jutted into the surf, slick with spray and choked with bladderwrack. Helen stepped to the starboard rail, her fingers stiff from fighting the wheel. The painter line of the little wooden tender was stiff with salt, its hemp biting into her knuckles as she threw a hitch around the iron cleat. She did not bother with a second tuck. The boat needed to ride clear for ten minutes while they drifted off the rocks and reset the head-sheet, nothing more.

A swell lifted under the stern, sudden and heavy as a shifting floor. The hemp slipped. It did not snap or fray; it simply spilled loose through the greasy horns of the cleat like unspooled ribbon. Helen lunged, her palm scraping bare timber, but the towline whistled over the coaming and vanished beneath the foam. Two seconds later, the grey sea hurled the tender broadside into the basalt wall. The strakes caved with the sharp, hollow crack of a stepping boot on a hearth-tile. Ribs splintered, planks folded back like peeled rind, and the splintered ruin was sucked out into the backwash before Helen could draw another breath.

"The skiff," Helen whispered, staring down into the frothing churn where only green bottom weed swayed.

Ruth stood beside the cabin companionway, one hand clamped hard against the teak handrail. Her knuckles were white, her grey eyes narrowed to slits against the sleet. She did not look at the water. She looked straight at Helen.

"You couldn't even drop a round turn," Ruth said, her voice dropping beneath the rumble of the breakers. "You never could be bothered to tie a thing down. Not the painter. Not Donald's seven days. Not him."

Helen felt the blood rush into her neck, hot against the wind. "It took a bad heave. A sea came aboard."

"A sea came aboard," Ruth mimicked, flat and venomous. She took a single step along the sodden deck, letting go of the handrail as if she no longer cared whether the boat kept her upright. "You spent three years running railway timetables in the south, Helen. Three years of telegraphs and postmarked postcards, while I lifted his legs into the zinc tub. While I washed his linen twice a day and emptied his chamber pots until my hands smelled of vinegar and rotten tallow. You blew into Skiff Cove in your city boots to sign Donald's papers, and you think you can steer us through Morren Head without even seating a cleat."

"I sent money," Helen said, her voice shaking, though she hated herself for the defense. "Every sovereign from the post office was mine, Ruth. You couldn't have bought the paraffin without me."

"Paraffin," Ruth spat, turning toward the cabin where the sealed tin urn rested beside the logbook on the shelf. "You bought candles to light the room while I sat beside the dying man. You bought yourself clean hands. And now we have no dinghy, no tender, and nowhere to go ashore if the hull opens up under us."

Helen gripped the spokes of the helm, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. The basalt wall dropped astern as the ebb tide caught the Shearwater's keel, dragging them back into the deep, restless swell of the passage. The wind whistled in the stays, thin and cold, and the boat drifted southward into the grey mist.

## Scene 4

The mist did not lift; it blackened. What began as a raw northern drift sharpened within minutes into a squall that flattened the chop into scudding sheets of white foam. The Shearwater pitched hard into the troughs, shuddering each time her bow struck green water. Ruth held fast to the doghouse grab rail, her eyes fixed on the stemhead where she had tried to ignore the hairline fracture. In the teeth of the wind, there was no more ignoring it. The starboard forestay sang with a high, unearthly hum, stretched taut as wire on a cheese-cutter.

The metal gave not with a groan, but with a crack like a pistol fired in an empty kitchen. The bronze tang sheared clean through its scarred grain. Relieved of its counter-stay, the spruce stick stood naked for one shuddering heartbeat before twenty feet of timber folded over the starboard deck. It struck the coaming with a dull thud, splintering the rail and dumping the mainsail into the foam like soaked damask torn from a banquet table.

The drag was instantaneous. Caught by the submerged canvas and the web of shrouds, the Shearwater heeled sharply to starboard, taking on water through the scuppers and refusing the rudder. At the helm, Helen was clinging to the spokes, her boots sliding in the oily bilge of the cockpit as grey crests poured across her ankles. She shouted something into the spray, but the wind took the words and scattered them like soot blown down an uncapped flue.

Ruth did not bother to answer. She scrambled to the companionway hatch, hauled the rigging knife and the flat-backed fire axe from the locker, and thrust the axe handle into Helen's wet hands. There was no time for resentment, no time for three years of dying old men, and no time for lost skiffs. If they did not cut the timber free before the next roller broadsided them, Arthur's urn would find the seabed five miles north of his secrets.

They worked on their knees in the wash. Helen swung the axe against the spruce stays and crushed lanyards, wild-eyed and gasping, while Ruth sawed furiously through the sodden manila sheets and jammed turnbuckles. Her knuckles scraped raw against the bronze fittings, the saltwater stinging like lye. Three times the hull dipped into the swell until the green sea rose to Ruth's waist, cold and heavy as lead, but they did not stop. With a final heave, Helen chopped through the remaining halyard, Ruth kicked the severed gooseneck clear of the deck, and the broken spar slid down into the dark water. The Shearwater righted herself with a violent roll, shaking free of the dead weight.

Ruth dragged Helen back into the cockpit, shoved the companionway drop-boards shut against the deluge, and threw the engine lever. The starter clattered twice, coughed a plume of oily stench into the spray, and then the single-cylinder diesel caught with a steady, hammering pulse. Helen took the helm without a word, her knuckles white on the brass rim, wiping salt and rain from her eyes with the sodden cuff of her sleeve.

Through the blinding downpour, they crawled south at four knots, the bare stump of the mast pointing at the rain like an amputated finger. Ahead, between two low spits of sodden scrub, opened the mouth of the creek. The water flattened under their keel, the wind dropped behind the bluffs, and the Shearwater drifted into the muddy gut of Low Water Creek, ruined, dismasted, and safe.

## Scene 5

Ruth kept her hand firm on the tiller, steering the dismasted hull well clear of the broad granite breakwater that sheltered the commercial wharves of Tern Bay. She did not aim for the coal piers or the shipyard basin. Instead, consulting the penciled figures in the margin of the log, she throttled back and swung the bow into Tern Bay Creek, a winding gut of brown water choked with yellow weed and salt grass. The engine sputtered, thumping unevenly on its mounts, but it pushed the battered boat steadily between the mudbanks.

Helen stood forward at the stump of the mast, her oilskins shining with greasy rain. "The logbook coordinates," Helen called back over the diesel's clatter. "They were never an offshore fueling stop. There isn't a bunker barge or a fuel dock for three miles."

"I know," Ruth said.

Ahead, tucked into a bend of the tidal channel where the alders dipped into the brine, a wooden landing appeared through the gray mist. It was a sagging pontoon of greening timbers, held fast by two iron-ringed piles. Nailed to the edge of the bull-rail was a small lead tally plate, corroded white at the rim, stamped with two plain initials: A. M.

Ruth laid the Shearwater alongside with a dull thud against the rotted rubber fenders. Helen caught an alder bough with the boathook, dropped a loop of braided hemp over the mooring pile, and turned. She did not climb onto the dock. Beyond the reed beds sat a low, weatherboard cottage with blind dormers, its front window green and wavy as old bottle-glass.

"You had better tell me what you agreed to in Skiff Cove," Ruth said, cutting the ignition.

Helen wiped rainwater from her cheek with the back of a raw thumb. "I took a deposit from a breaker at the commercial docks," she confessed, her voice thin. "Two hundred pounds cash in hand. He has a slip waiting. I meant to sell the Shearwater the hour we made landfall, pay Arthur's timber accounts, and settle the shop debts before the bailiff moved in."

"You were going to let them scrap her behind my back," Ruth said.

"We were drowning in his arrears, Ruth. The mast is firewood, and Donald's seaworthiness waiver expires before the week is out. What else was there to sell?"

Ruth reached into the dark cabin and lifted Arthur's ash urn from the shelf behind the companionway. The brass cylinder was cold and solid in her hands. "Look at the cottage, Helen. He didn't steer us here to save us from his debts. On his deathbed, while I sat beside his blankets, he didn't speak of your shop or the harbourmaster. He gave me these coordinates and made me promise to carry him to this door. A woman named Clara. A second household he kept hidden in this creek for twenty years, cloaked in false entries in his coastal fuel book. He wanted us to carry his remains to her step and dump his ghost in her lap."

Helen stared up the overgrown path, where the untended lawn lay drowned under nettles and sea-plantain, dark and neglected like tarnished velvet curtains shut against the day. "He wanted a spectacle," Helen murmured.

"He wanted us to do his penance for him," Ruth said. "And leave her to weep over what he left behind."

Helen stepped onto the floating dock, the sodden wood sinking slightly beneath her boots, and held out both hands. Ruth passed the canister to her. Together, they walked through the sea-grass to the edge of the unkempt lawn. Helen worked her fingers around the threaded brass lid. The seam was seized with salt, but Ruth clamped her grip beside Helen's, and with a harsh, grating screech of metal on metal, the cap broke free.

Neither woman spoke an invocation. Ruth took the vessel, tilted the mouth toward the rising breeze, and tipped it wide. The ashes spilled in a heavy, chalky billow, scattering like cold sweepings from an empty hearth across the briars, the dandelion stalks, and the wild dock. Not a grain touched the flagstones of the porch. A squall of rain followed, beating the gray dust down into the mud until the soil swallowed it whole.

Helen set the empty canister under her arm and looked back toward the channel. "The breaker can come looking for his deposit," she said evenly. "We have fuel enough to clear the headland."

"And three days left on the waiver," Ruth said.

They cast off the lines, started the chugging diesel, and reversed the Shearwater out of the muddy gut into open water. With the bare stump of the mast lashed down and the wind at their quarters, they ran northward through the clearing weather. By the following afternoon, the blunt bow pushed through the entrance of Skiff Cove, gliding into the familiar stillness of their home harbor, where they tied up against the weathered pilings safe at last.

## Scene 6

The tide was barely half-slack when they walked the boat down from the pilings toward the cradle at Skiff Cove, where the shingle bar curved like an old elbow against the grey chop. Helen kept one hand on the reverse lever, gentling the chugging diesel until the timber skids groaned beneath the keel. Three hours remained on the yellow slipway notice pinned to the harbour office wall. It had been seven days to the afternoon, almost to the turn of the glass.

Donald Crane, whose bad hip had measured out forty years of harbour dues, hobbled down the concrete ramp with his clipboard under one arm. He did not look surprised to see them, though he squinted hard at the empty deck where the mast had stood. When he reached the water's edge, he spat into the weed and tapped the rusted grease-gun against his thigh.

"You took your time," Donald said. "The impound warrant was drawn. It sits on the blotter with the seal dry."

"She’s in before the tide went," Helen told him. Her hands on the wheel were stiff with dried salt and engine black, but her grip was steady. "The winch is yours, Donald. Take her up."

The iron drum turned with a shriek that set the gulls off the fish-gutters. The steel hawser crept dripping out of the water like the frayed hem of black silk, hauling the cradle up the timber runners inch by inch until the scarred bilge cleared the foam. Through the wheelhouse windows, the afternoon sun cast a thin yellow glaze across the dials, dim and yellow as a tallow candle guttering in an empty jar.

Donald walked the length of the hull, his knuckles rapping against the strakes. He paused at the severed forestay, shaking his head at the splintered pine stump. "She's uncertified for open water. You know the rule. Registry hold stays fast until she's surveyed, or she goes to the breaker’s hammer."

"She stays on the hard," Ruth said, stepping out onto the gunwale and hopping down into the gravel. She wiped her palms on her canvas apron. "Put the drydock rent down in both our names. Half to Helen, half to me. We’ll find a stick of fir before the winter sets in, and we’ll plane it ourselves."

Helen looked down at her sister, then across the deck to the empty space where their father's urn had rested beside the compass. The Tern Bay deposit would have to be mailed back with an apology and a short explanation, but that was paper work, and paper work could wait. "Split down the middle," Helen said to Donald. "Berth fees, timber, and the yard crane. Lift the seizure hold, Donald. She isn’t going anywhere."

Donald grunted, though his mouth twitched at the corner. He uncapped his stub of pencil, drew a thick line through the provisional impound entry, and initialed the margin. "Mondays for the slip rent," he said, turning back toward the warmth of his shed. "And don't leave your shavings on my slip."

From the stovepipe of the watchman's shack, a plume of banked peat embers rose straight into the quiet coastal air, fading to grey before it met the clouds. Helen climbed down the ladder to the shingle, standing shoulder to shoulder with Ruth while the water drained in thin rivulets from the boat's deadwood, leaving the Shearwater settled, upright, and home.
