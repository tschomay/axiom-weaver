# Engagement and Believability — The Borrowed Boat

**Score: 3/5** — The voyage (Scenes 1–4) builds real, well-planted pressure and I was leaning in, but the sisters' reckoning happens offstage and the last two scenes resolve on choices the story never prepared for, so the ending goes slack.

## Findings

### 1. [major] The sisters' reconciliation happens between scenes
- **Where:** Scene 5 → Scene 6
- **Quote:** Scene 5: "She climbed over the washboard onto the wooden slipway and did not look back as her boots struck the gravel road." / Scene 6: "She stepped up beside Cleo, her shoulder brushing her sister's arm without pulling away."
- **Problem:** The estrangement is the story's emotional spine, and Scene 3 sharpens it into an accusation of theft ("Did you take his money?"). Ruth walks off alone at the end of Scene 5, then turns up on the carrier with Cleo, reconciled. Ruth never confronts Cleo for hiding a half-brother and cutting the log, and we never see the thaw. The payoff the reader was waiting for is skipped.
- **Reader needed:** The moment on the slipway or the carrier where Ruth deals with Cleo's secret and chooses to stand beside her.

### 2. [major] Cleo's plan doesn't hold together
- **Where:** Scene 1 and Scene 5
- **Quote:** Scene 1: "\"She will run,\" Cleo said. \"And father goes to Lowood Bight.\"" / Scene 5: "He didn't want the Cormorant sold to the timber yard, Ruth. He wanted her brought here, to David."
- **Problem:** Cleo has known for weeks that Arthur wanted the boat given to David. Yet she backs the sale, fights to keep the engine running for the buyer's clause, and never says what she meant to do on arrival. It's unclear whether she was steering toward the sale or toward David. The reveal makes her earlier behaviour harder to understand, not easier.
- **Reader needed:** A hint of what Cleo was really aiming for (stalling the sale, hoping to buy time, meaning to confess at Lowood), so the Scene 5 reveal reframes her actions instead of contradicting them.

### 3. [major] The resolution turns on a longing introduced in the last scene
- **Where:** Scene 6
- **Quote:** "For ten years she had thought that bench was the only future permitted to her. She looked down at her blistered palms... and felt nothing of the old binding weight."
- **Problem:** Cleo's final choice, refusing the workshop, is the story's closing beat, but her tie to the bench and her sense of being trapped by it are never mentioned before this paragraph. The refusal has nothing to push against, so it lands as a statement rather than a release.
- **Reader needed:** An earlier moment showing that the workshop or the water is both Cleo's inheritance and her cage.

### 4. [minor] Toby changes his mind too easily
- **Where:** Scene 6 (against Scene 1)
- **Quote:** "His voice had softened, shedding its edge of official greed. \"...I could renew the tenancy in your name. We could wipe the old slate clean\""
- **Problem:** The man who pried the storm anchor off the deck over twelve pounds four is ready, a few lines later, to wipe the debt and offer a cheap lease. The news about Martha Miller doesn't obviously explain the change. The debt and bailiff threat that loomed through the voyage simply disappear.
- **Reader needed:** A reason for Toby's change (loyalty to Arthur, guilt over the anchor, his own interest in keeping the shed let).

### 5. [minor] The missed slack window is summarised rather than lost on the page
- **Where:** Scene 4 (against Scene 3)
- **Quote:** Scene 3: "We have twenty minutes before Gull Shoal turns against us." / Scene 4: "The twenty-minute shoal tide window had slipped past while she was choking on bilge brine off Crow Point"
- **Problem:** The deadline Toby set up so carefully is lost in a single sentence of recap, straight after a line suggesting there was still time. The sisters also run a reef they know is deadly on the ebb, and nobody weighs holding off.
- **Reader needed:** Seeing the window close, or seeing the sisters choose to run it anyway and why.

### 6. [minor] The ashes are handed off without a word
- **Where:** Scene 5 (against Scene 1)
- **Quote:** Scene 1: "And father goes to Lowood Bight. We agreed on that much" / Scene 5: "\"He's yours,\" Ruth said. \"The boat, the debt, and the dust. Take all of it.\""
- **Problem:** The quest to take Arthur's ashes where he asked ends with the urn passed to David, and neither sister reacts to not scattering them. Ruth's jump from "He had no business in Lowood" to giving everything away takes a few paragraphs of interior aphorism ("To hold the urn was to keep carrying the corpse of a dead man's neglect").
- **Reader needed:** Ruth's gesture framed against the promise they set out to keep, so giving up the urn is felt as a cost.

### 7. [nit] Scene 6 re-tells what the reader already knows
- **Where:** Scene 6
- **Quote:** "We gave the vessel to his son... David Miller. He lives by the lime kilns in Lowood Bight with Martha. He had Arthur's brass key in his pocket and Arthur's nose on his face."
- **Problem:** Cleo repeats the Scene 5 reveal to Toby almost point for point, which slows the final scene.
- **Reader needed:** Only Toby's reaction, with the recap cut short.

## What works
- Scene 1 plants every later danger in plain sight: the contract clause, the lost storm anchor and the slack window. "You have a twenty-minute slack window, not a minute more, before the ebb turns" pays off with real dread in Scene 4.
- The squall is taut, physical and decided by Cleo's own action: "Cleo sawed at the three-strand hemp with three fierce, tearing strokes. The line parted with a sound like a rifle crack." (Scene 2).
- Escalation across the voyage is well stacked: squall, then overheating, then the razored log pages ("Neatly sheared away along the binding with a razor, leaving only a row of ragged white stubs", Scene 3), with each crisis adding personal pressure on top of the physical danger.

## Out of lane
Cleo cut the halyard and dropped the sail in Scene 2, yet in Scene 5 "The sail hung limp from the pine spar" (consistency).
