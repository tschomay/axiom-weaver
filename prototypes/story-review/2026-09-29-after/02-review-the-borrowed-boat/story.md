The workshop smelled of pine shavings, stale tallow, and cold stove ash. On the bench lay Arthur's bronze ash urn, sealed down with black pitch at the rim, beside the heavy linen folds of the conditional bill of sale.

Ruth flattened the contract with the heel of her hand. Two years in the city had made her sharp-featured and crisp about money, and she looked at the boatyard as though it were already lumber for kindling. "The yard in Lowood will break her up for her copper nails if we ask them to," Ruth said. "I am not nursing an old dead man's hull for sentiment. The clause is plain enough, Cleo. If the motor fails, or if the drive shaft bends on a pebble, the buyer keeps the deposit and walks away. We take the Cormorant under her own power, or we get nothing."

Cleo set her hand on the cold bronze urn. Arthur had spent his final winter coughing into a wool blanket by the dying hearth, whispering his last plea between rattling breaths, and she had spent three months washing his spoons. "She will run," Cleo said. "And father goes to Lowood Bight. We agreed on that much before you took the train from the capital."

Ruth gave a dry snort. She snatched the parchment, slid it into her oilskin sleeve, and turned toward the slipway. "Then bring him along. But if the engine seizes, I am turning the helm straight into the nearest breaker."

Outside, the morning fog clung to the tidal mud. The Cormorant rested on her greased timber cradle at the edge of Skiff Haven, looking narrow and long in the grey light. Cleo carried the heavy urn down the ramp, her forearms aching under the deadweight, while Ruth tossed the canvas hawsers onto the foredeck.

Toby Fenn stepped out from behind the winch shed, his oilskin trousers stiff with salt. He was arthritic, slow-footed, and remarkably stubborn when an account sat open on his slate. He planted one thick hand against the Cormorant’s stemhead, blocking the skid.

"Not yet, girls," Toby said. He spat into the seaweed. "Arthur went into the ground owing Skiff Haven three full seasons on the slip. Twelve pounds four, counting the chain grease and the winter wedges. You don’t slide her into the reach until the ledger clears."

Ruth’s mouth thinned into a pale wire. "The estate has no coin until Lowood. You know that, Toby Fenn."

"I know what the slate says," Toby replied evenly. His rheumy eyes drifted over the deck, settling on the heavy galvanized storm anchor resting beside the bitts. It was sixty pounds of forged iron, forged to bite deep into bottom-mud when an offshore gale blew up. "The iron stays behind. I can sell that anchor to the trawlers by noon for nine pounds, and call the rest Arthur's bad luck."

"We need that ground tackle," Cleo said, stepping between him and the deck. "Grey Reach runs heavy this time of year."

"Then keep her off the shoals," Toby said. He unhooked the lashing with a pair of pliers, hefted the heavy iron fluke over the gunwale, and let it thud into the gravel. Cleo looked down at the empty bow ring, then into the dark locker where only their spare kedging anchor lay—an undersized, light fluke meant for holding a rowboat in a sheltered cove, useless against a surging tide.

Ruth was already in the cockpit, thumbing the starter button. The diesel sputtered once, threw a plume of oily grey smoke, and settled into a wet, thrumming idle. "Get aboard, Cleo," Ruth called, not bothering to look back. "The tide won't wait for your bargaining."

Cleo clambered over the coaming, clutching the urn to her ribs. Toby knocked the wooden chock from the slip runner with a mallet. As the Cormorant began to slide down the greasy timber toward the grey chop, he leaned over the rub-rail.

"Mind the clock at Gull Shoal, Cleo," Toby warned, raising his voice above the churn. "That passage isn't water to dally in. You have a twenty-minute slack window, not a minute more, before the ebb turns and the rip tide boils white over the reef. Miss the slack, and the rocks will peel that hull like an orange."

The Cormorant splashed into the cold wash of Grey Reach. The timber hummed underfoot as Ruth engaged the clutch, steering their bow into the mist, leaving Toby and the heavy iron fluke on the diminishing shore.

## Scene 2

The Reach was an ungenerous stretch of water, a wide trough of slate and brackish swell caught between the black shale cliffs and the outer shoals. There was no kindness in it even at midday. The wind came hard off the gap, tearing the white foam clean off the chop and flinging it against the timber.

Ruth had insisted on taking the helm. She stood braced against the cockpit grating in her tailored city coat, her boots skidding on the wet fir, handling the wheel as though it were a wagon brake. She fought every swell instead of letting the hull lift, jerking the rudder back and forth with grim, furious concentration. Down in the cabin, Arthur's bronze urn sat wedged behind the companionway fiddle next to the buyer's packet, quiet and indifferent to the pitching.

Cleo had raised the mainsail twenty minutes out of the haven to steady their roll and save the fuel they would need for the reef. Toby’s warning remained fresh in her ears: slack water at Gull Shoal lasted twenty minutes, and once the ebb caught them, no diesel engine could fight the rips.

Then the squall fell on them without a breath of warning.

It was not a gradual freshening. It dropped out of the low cloud like an iron weight, roaring over the starboard quarter. Cleo reached for the mainsheet to spill the air, but the line snubbed instantly, wedged tight in the throat of the upper block by an old riding turn.

The wind caught the jammed canvas and levered the boat flat. The Cormorant heeled with a sickening lurch, laying her port gunwale clean under the sea. Green water rushed over the coaming in an icy flood, pouring knee-deep across the cockpit sole.

"Luff her!" Cleo shouted against the roar, calling for Ruth to turn the bow into the wind and spill the canvas. "Turn into the weather, Ruth! Turn her!"

Ruth did not move. Her fingers were frozen white around the wheel spokes. Her jaw hung open, her eyes wide, staring blankly at the frothing grey sea rising over the rail. Terror had turned her to stone.

The ash boom dipped into the surf. Forward, the pine mast bent in a lethal arc, creaking under the leverage. A boat did not need much time to roll completely over once the gunwale went under, and a snapped spar would puncture the cabin top or foul the propeller below, voiding the contract and their lives in the same stroke.

Cleo dropped to the deck.

She did not waste breath screaming at Ruth. She threw herself flat against the wash, clawing her way up the canted foredeck. The timber was slick as grease. Spray struck her cheek like shattered glass, blinding her in one eye as she crawled, belly to the planks, digging her fingernails into the caulked seams. Every heave of the Reach threatened to toss her into the churning froth below the rail.

She reached the mast partner, wrapped one leg around the pin rail, and dragged her rigging knife from her belt.

The halyard hummed with tension, hard as an iron bar. Cleo sawed at the three-strand hemp with three fierce, tearing strokes.

The line parted with a sound like a rifle crack.

The gaff plunged down. The heavy canvas collapsed into a sodden heap across the cabin roof, and without the lever of the wind, the Cormorant righted herself with a violent wallow. A torrent of grey foam sluiced through the scuppers back into the sea.

Cleo lay against the coaming for several seconds, coughing brine, battered and saltwater-chapped to the bone. When she crawled aft into the cockpit, Ruth was slumped over the lee bulwark, retching into the wash, shivering so hard the wheel trembled beneath her unmoving palms.

## Scene 3

Ruth wiped bile from her lip with the cuff of her sleeve. Her stomach still rolled with every slap of the chop, but the boat was level, churning past the black spine of Crow Point under the thrum of the diesel.

Then the instrument panel began to shriek.

A shrill, unbroken whistle pierced the cockpit, followed by the acrid stink of scorching paint. The temperature needle pegged hard into the red. Steam hissed through the engine hatch seams.

"Kill the throttle!" Cleo shouted from the deck, scrambling aft on hands and knees. "Kill it, Ruth!"

Ruth yanked the brass lever back. The engine sputtered into a rough idle, shaking the floorboards like an old dog shaking off river muck.

"The raw-water intake is choked," Cleo said, wrenching up the deck hatch. A billow of wet heat broke over them. "Driftweed. Must have sucked a mat of it into the sea-chest." She threw off the brass wing nuts of the sea-chest, the grated intake chamber set below the waterline that fed cold brine through the cooling jacket. Without it, the cylinder head would crack within three minutes, and the buyer's contract in Ruth's oilskin pouch would become scrap paper.

Cleo reached into the companionway locker, tossing aside oil rags and tackle until she hauled out Arthur’s canvas tool roll. With it came his blue-bound logbook, tumbling out onto the cockpit bench.

"Take the wheel," Cleo ordered, dropping down beside the manifold with the spanner. "Keep her nose into the swell or we’ll roll again."

Ruth took the wheel. Her fingers were stiff with cold, but her gaze snagged on the logbook sprawled open on the teak. A salt gust fluttered the leaves. Ruth pinned the book with one hand while keeping the spokes steady with her hip.

Arthur had kept books for forty years. He recorded fuel, tide, pints of kerosene, every brass washer. But where the final season’s records belonged—the months he spent berthed at Lowood while his health failed—the pages were gone. Neatly sheared away along the binding with a razor, leaving only a row of ragged white stubs.

Down in the bilge, metal clanged. A vicious hiss of superheated steam barked out, and Cleo gasped in pain.

"Cleo?" Ruth called, staring down at the shorn threads.

Cleo emerged from the hatchway clutching her left wrist against her ribs. The skin across her palms and knuckles was mottled red and weeping, blistered from scalding engine brine that had erupted when she pried the strainer cap free. A slimy clot of kelp and eelgrass dangled from her right fingers like frayed brocade.

"Intake is clear," Cleo panted, dumping the weed over the rail. "Restart the pump. We didn't throw a rod."

Ruth did not reach for the starter key. She held up the open logbook, thumbing the excised stubs. "Where are the Lowood sheets, Cleo?"

Cleo froze, cradling her blistered hand. "What?"

"Five hundred pounds of father's railway pension," Ruth said, her voice sharpening. "Gone from his account before he died in Lowood. Toby Fenn seized his storm anchor because the berth went unpaid, yet Father supposedly had savings. You stayed with him in Lowood those last three weeks. You cut these pages out."

"Ruth, start the engine," Cleo said, her face pale beneath the grease and salt. "We have twenty minutes before Gull Shoal turns against us."

"Did you take his money?"

"Start the engine, damn you!"

Ruth turned the key. The starter ground twice, caught, and settled into a wet, steady throb. But as Ruth looked at her sister’s raw hands, the suspicion hardened, cold and unmoving.

## Scene 4

No words passed between them as the headland fell away and the Cormorant chugged steadily toward Gull Shoal, her restarted engine vibrating through Cleo's blistered palms. The sun hung three fingers above the western firs, flat and yellow as a copper coin, and Cleo did not need Toby Fenn’s pocket almanac to read the reckoning. The twenty-minute shoal tide window had slipped past while she was choking on bilge brine off Crow Point, leaving only the undersized kedge anchor to bite against an ebb that had now turned in earnest.

Ahead, Gull Shoal announced itself not with a roar, but with a heavy, sucking murmur. Grey Reach was draining through the granite teeth of the reef like water from a tipped tub. Yellow foam gathered in greasy ropes across the rip tide, spinning in widening funnels that drew the Cormorant’s bow toward the black fangs of the outer skerry.

"Cleo," Ruth said. The chill had left her voice, replaced by the thin, brittle edge of outright terror. "She isn’t answering the wheel. The current has us."

Cleo scrambled forward on her knees, salt stinging her blistered knuckles. The diesel pounded under the deckboards, its rhythm frantic and futile against four knots of outgoing surge. The channel was narrowing down to thirty yards of boiled foam between two shelves of iron-grey stone. If the current took them broadside onto the ledges, the pine planking would open like kindling.

"Take her out of gear!" Cleo shouted over the roar of the boil. "Cut the throttle! We’ll have to drop the pick!"

Ruth yanked the brass lever back into neutral. The engine settled to an uneasy idle. Cleo unhitched the painter and hauled the spare kedge anchor over the bulwark, letting its rusty tines plunge into the froth. In Skiff Haven, Fenn had taken their sixty-pound storm anchor to settle their father’s slip dues; this light kedging hook was all Toby had left them. She paid out fifteen fathoms of rope until the line snapped taut against the samson post.

The anchor dragged. Cleo felt the stutter through the timber under her knees—the light fluke skittering helplessly over slick shale and weed, unable to bite without the lost anchor’s weight to pin it down. The boat barely checked her slide. The outgoing race caught the stern and swung the Cormorant sideways, broadside toward the granite ledge. Ten feet away, the dark rock reared out of the foam like the blunt snout of a drowning beast.

"It’s not holding!" Ruth screamed, gripping the wheel with both hands, her knuckles white. "We’re going on the rocks!"

Cleo didn’t shout back. There was no time for blame, no time for the five hundred pounds, no time for contracts. She flung herself aft across the cockpit, shoved Ruth aside, and seized the heavy iron reverse lever with both blistered palms.

She slammed it hard astern and opened the throttle to the stop.

The diesel shrieked. Black smoke belched from the exhaust, smelling of scorched cinders, as the propeller bit violently into the foam. The boat shuddered from stem to stern, fighting the race, dragging her belly against the current just inches shy of the granite teeth. For two heartbeats the hull hung suspended between the surge and the screws.

Then came the strike.

A sickening, hollow crack slammed through the floorboards. The bronze blades clipped the underwater rock shelf. The engine gave one high, hysterical scream of unburdened revs before a sharp, metallic snap rang through the bilge. The drive shaft sheared in two. The propeller went silent, tumbling dead into the weed, and the engine died into an oily silence.

Yet the dying thrust had broken the current’s grip. The Cormorant spun once in the eddy, scraped her oak keel along the submerged shelf with a long, groaning shudder, and slid through the narrow gut of the reef. The tearing roar of the rip water dropped behind them like a shut door.

Borne by the last of her dying momentum, the boat drifted into the quiet, glassy shallows of Lowood Bight. The shoreline curved about them, sheltered and still, the evening mists pooling white over mudflats and timber jetties. Cleo leaned against the coaming, her burned hands shaking so badly she could not ball them into fists.

Behind her, the engine box ticked in the cold air, dead and ruined. Ruth stared down through the hatch at the severed stump of the drive shaft, then reached into her pocket and touched the folded corner of the bill of sale. Cleo had saved their skins, but the buyer’s clause was broken, the deposit was gone, and the Cormorant was a derelict.

## Scene 5

The hull scraped against the green timber of the Lowood slipway with a long, hollow shiver. In the shallows, the pine bottom grated upon shingle, then settled into the black mud of the bight. The sail hung limp from the pine spar, useless now that the wind had died behind the headland.

A young man stood waiting at the high-water mark, an oilskin coat pulled over broad shoulders. He stepped into the wash without hesitating, water foaming past his sea boots, and caught the bow painter before Ruth could lift the coil from the foredeck. In his right hand, strung upon a loop of tarred twine, swung the brass notched spade of Arthur’s spare workshop key.

Ruth knew the cut of that key. It had hung beside the clock in the Skiff Haven kitchen for thirty winters until the day Arthur packed his chest and left for the western reaches.

"You made the shoal," the man said. His voice was low, carrying Arthur’s flat coastal drawl and the hard vowels of the outer sounds. "He said you’d bring her through if Cleo had the helm."

Ruth looked from the key to the boy’s face. The line of his jaw and the heavy, hooded grey eyes were not a stranger’s; they were Arthur Varner’s features, smoothed of thirty years of drink and grease. She turned sharply toward the cockpit hatch. "Cleo. Who is this?"

Cleo sat on the engine casing, her blistered palms curled in her lap. She did not look up at the slipway. "His name is David Miller," she said, the words coming flat and heavy as lead sinkers. "Arthur wrote to me three weeks before he took to his bed. He didn't want the Cormorant sold to the timber yard, Ruth. He wanted her brought here, to David. The boat, the tools, and what was left of him."

"He was our father," Ruth said, the bile rising cold in her throat. "He had no business in Lowood."

"He had twenty years of business here," Cleo whispered. "A house behind the lime kilns, a woman named Martha Miller, and a boy who grew up in his workshop while we were waiting for pension money that was already spent. The five hundred pounds didn't go to bad timber. It went to David’s schooling and the slipway lease."

A third figure approached along the timber jetty—a man in a stiff woollen frock coat, carrying an iron gauge and a clip of papers. The marine surveyor did not glance at David or the sisters. He stepped down onto the gunwale with the weary precision of a clerk inspecting a dead mule. He dropped to his knees by the opened hatch, leaned an oil lantern into the dark, and tapped the severed stump of the drive shaft with his brass rule.

"Drive shaft sheared clean at the coupling," the surveyor announced, dusting rust flakes from his cuff. "The bilge box is cracked, and the gland is burnt to charcoal. The mechanical integrity clause requires an operable engine under its own power upon delivery. The purchase is void, Miss Varner. The deposit returns to the timber syndicate, less my survey fee."

He produced the conditional bill of sale from his folder, drew a thick stroke of red ink across the signature line, and handed the defaced sheet to Ruth. The paper was cold and damp in her fingers.

Everything Ruth had calculated on the kitchen table in Skiff Haven—the settlement of the mooring arrears, the rail fare inland, the clean slate purchased with the vessel’s scrap value—evaporated into the salt fog. She had starved through three winters to keep the council from the cottage, while Arthur had built a second hearth across the water, feeding a boy who bore another woman's name.

"She's fouled with driftweed and the shaft is gone," David said quietly, looking down into the engine pit. "The timber syndicate would break her up for ribs and kindling before the tide turns. But the oak keel is sound. I have the spare shaft Arthur turned last winter in the shed. I can haul her up the slipway before the ebb and rebuild her."

Ruth stood by the mast, her fingers gripping the cold bronze urn. The metal was heavy and inert, sealed with black pitch that smelt faintly of turpentine. She could refuse. She could carry the urn down the road, leave the wrecked hull to rot on the county flats, and force the bailiffs to pick through Arthur’s debts until nothing remained for anyone. It would be justice, bitter and thin as sea water.

She looked at David. He held out his rough hands, the nails split from cedar caulking, waiting without pleading. He was Arthur’s blood, Arthur’s secret, and Arthur’s heir, standing upon the mud of a life Arthur had kept hidden from the world.

To hold the urn was to keep carrying the corpse of a dead man's neglect. To let it go was to walk away with empty pockets and two bruised hands.

Ruth drew a breath of the salt air, stepped across the thwart, and laid Arthur’s bronze urn into David Miller’s outstretched palms. She let her fingers pull away from the cold metal without looking down.

"He's yours," Ruth said. "The boat, the debt, and the dust. Take all of it."

She climbed over the washboard onto the wooden slipway and did not look back as her boots struck the gravel road.

## Scene 6
<!-- card: scene_06_the_refusal -->

The deck of the coastal carrier was slick with coal grit and diesel scum. Cleo stood by the salt-stained rail as the thrum of the engine eased, watching the familiar slate quays of Skiff Haven Harbour slide into view beneath a low ceiling of grey cloud. It was the same sour basin they had fled at daybreak, smelled of rotting bladderwrack, bilge water, and rusted iron.

Ruth did not speak. She sat hunched on a bench by the wheelhouse, her sodden skirts tucked beneath her knees and her bare hands locked tight in her lap. The cold wind off the estuary had bitten the color from her cheeks, leaving behind only the dull grease of exhaustion. When the carrier bumped against the pontoon timbers and the deckhand threw a spring line ashore, Ruth rose without a word. Her stride was unsteady, but she did not fall.

Cleo jumped down after her onto the creaking floats. Standing by the empty timber slip where the Cormorant had rotted through three rainy winters was Toby Fenn, the arthritic harbourmaster who had dogged their departure. He had propped Arthur's forfeited storm anchor—sixty pounds of pitted iron—against a piling, his swollen knuckles resting on its stock. In the dim afternoon light, the brass buttons of his wool coat caught a dull gleam, steady as tallow wick and candlelight.

"Back already," Toby said. He did not move from the post. His rheumy eyes scanned the grey sweep of water behind them, searching for the green sheerline that was not there. "Where did you leave her? On Gull Shoal?"

"Lowood," Cleo said. Her voice sounded thin to her own ears, cracked by brine and fumes, but she kept her chin up. "She's grounded in the bight."

Toby grunted, rubbing his hip where the damp always settled. "And the buyer? Did he sign the bill? Because there is twelve pounds four still standing in my ledger, and I don't intend to wipe it out for a pair of girls who couldn't keep a pine keel off the granite."

"There is no buyer," Ruth said. It was the first time she had spoken since they boarded the carrier, and her tone was flat, hollowed out of all its old sharpness. She stepped up beside Cleo, her shoulder brushing her sister's arm without pulling away. "The drive shaft snapped before we cleared the race. The contract is void."

Toby looked from Ruth's blank face to Cleo's scalded hands. He let out a slow whistle through his teeth. "Then Arthur's debts sit with Arthur's kin. That boat was all you had between you and the bailiff's lock on the workshop door."

"The boat isn't ours," Cleo said simply. "And neither are the ashes."

The harbourmaster frowned, his heavy eyebrows knitting together. "What nonsense is this?"

"We gave the vessel to his son," Cleo said. "David Miller. He lives by the lime kilns in Lowood Bight with Martha. He had Arthur's brass key in his pocket and Arthur's nose on his face. The Cormorant sits in his creek now, and Arthur's urn is on his shelf. Arthur maintained a second family there for twenty years, Toby. If you want your twelve pounds four, take the morning packet across the channel and ask his boy for it."

Toby stared at her. His mouth worked silently for a moment, the stubborn defiance draining from his stooped shoulders. The water lapped softly against the pilings, rhythmic and indifferent. "Martha Miller," he muttered at last, shaking his head. "Lord above. He always did keep two logs for every voyage."

He looked down at the iron anchor between his boots, then glanced up at the brick shed that housed Arthur's old bench and tools. The glass of its transom was dark, layered in cobwebs.

"The lease on the shed lapses at sundown," Toby said quietly. His voice had softened, shedding its edge of official greed. "You know the lathe is sound, Cleo. You've got your father's hands for joinery, even if you lack his cunning. I could renew the tenancy in your name. We could wipe the old slate clean and call it ten shillings a month to start fresh."

Cleo looked at the darkened shed. She could smell the familiar ghost of it from where she stood: cedar shavings, linseed oil, and old tobacco. For ten years she had thought that bench was the only future permitted to her. She looked down at her blistered palms, raw and stinging from engine brine, and felt nothing of the old binding weight.

"No," Cleo said. She did not hesitate. "Keep the anchor, Toby. Sell the tools to pay the slip fees. We're done with the water."

Toby watched her for a beat, seeing something in her set jaw that brooked no bargaining, and gave a brief, solitary nod. He stepped aside from the gangway.

Cleo turned away from the tide and touched Ruth's cold sleeve. Ruth glanced at her, startled, then let out a long breath and fell into step at her side. Together, the two sisters walked off the pontoon and headed up the gravel track, leaving the harbour and their father behind them.
