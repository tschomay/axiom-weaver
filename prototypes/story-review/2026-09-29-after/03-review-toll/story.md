The paper was dry, stiffened with government sizing, and it rattled in Arthur Finch's hand before the wind could take it.

He stood on the flat river-slate of the porch, his oilskin coat buttoned to the collar against the spray blowing up from the gorge. Beyond the picket fence, two work gangs had already begun sledge-hammering surveyor stakes into the turf along the garden ditch, stringing yellow rope across the clover. The condemned boundary was marked in lead ink across the vellum he held out: the valley flooding order, stamped with the seal of the provincial water commission, declaring Blackwood Basin an emergency catchment.

"The draft is drawn on the treasury at Oakhaven," Arthur said, his voice flat, drained of any will to quarrel. He reached into his satchel for the voucher of compensation. "It covers the acreage, the timber stands, and the foundation timbering. You have less than an hour before the weir locks release."

Teresa did not put out her hand. Her fingers stayed tucked against the rough wool of her apron, cold and immovable as river boulders. "Keep your silver paper, Arthur. You brought your chainmen and your cofferdams, but you never bought the dirt under this sill, and you never will."

She let the notice slip from his gloved fingers. It fell into the puddle gathering on the threshold, ink bleeding purple into the grey rain.

A dull, iron thud shuddered through the soles of her boots. Half a mile south, where the narrows had been choked with crushed limestone and timber cribbing, the diversion gates dropped into their iron channels. The river did not pause; it backed into the basin like dark ale rising in an earthen jug. Muddy froth spilled over the cabbage patch, drowning the row ends and licking at the cellar grating.

Inside the hall, Myra gripped Teresa's shoulder with the blunt strength of a woman used to dragging drowned sheep from the freshets. "The road is under at the ford, Terry. If we linger for the sideboard, we go down with the floorboards."

"Let it come," Teresa whispered, but Myra gave her no choice.

Myra hauled her backward through the kitchen door, tearing wool against the latch, dragging her up the goat path that climbed the eastern shoulder of the ridge. Teresa stumbled in the ferns, looking back over her arm. The water met the porch posts with a low, gulping sound. It swallowed the rain barrel, poured through the lower sashes with a shatter of glass, and crept up the shake roof until only the brick chimney stood out against the brown boil. By the time they reached the crest of the cart track, thirty fathoms of slate-colored flood had sealed the valley from ridge to ridge, silencing every sheep bell and creek bed.

Down the southern track, the red roof tiles and church tower of Oakhaven lay huddled against the mist, safe behind the high diversion levies where Arthur and his engineers oversaw the impoundment works.

"We can hire rooms near the tannery," Myra said, breathless, wiping cold spit and rain from her cheeks as she pulled Teresa along the road toward the borough gates. "There will be laundry work, or the infirmary kitchen. We must sleep somewhere dry tonight, Teresa. The valley is gone."

"It is not gone," Teresa said. She stopped at the milestone that marked the Oakhaven town line, her boots soaked through with valley clay, her gaze fixed backward on the gray expanse drowning the hollow. "Let them pile thirty fathoms on the orchard. Let them hold the river until the timbers rot. That water will yield the farm back, Myra. I will see the lake emptied until our hearth stones stand dry under open sky."

## Scene 2

Twenty years had passed since the river drowned the orchard, yet the High Reach Dam Control Room smelled perpetually of wet slate and stagnant lime, its vaulted embrasures set deep within thirty feet of monolithic granite that vibrated with the muffled, continuous thunder of the impounded basin.

Arthur Finch sat behind a desk of oiled walnut that was neatly piled with survey ledgers and brass-cornered intake registers. His temples were iron-gray now, his posture settled into the stiff, rounded stoop of a civil functionary whose life had been measured out in cubic yards of poured concrete and water levels logged at dawn. When he looked up, his pale eyes drifted across Teresa’s face—scarred along the jawline by grease-burns from years of apprentice work in the lower canal yards—and found there only the dull competence of an aging mechanic.

He did not know her.

"The board requires the hydraulic seal certificate signed in triplicate, Mistress Vane," Finch said, his voice dry and hollowed out by the vast masonry spaces of the gatehouse. He dipped a steel pen into a black inkwell and slid the heavy linen sheet across the green blotter. "The diversion tunnels beneath the eastern abutment have developed heavy seepage along the pressure joints. The district cannot afford another season of unmonitored settlement."

Teresa took the pen. The metal was cold in her calloused fingers, but her hand remained level as she inscribed her name across the official seal, setting her mark beneath the stamped seal of the Imperial Water Authority. For two decades she had studied the joints, the valves, and the immense sluice chambers of the riverworks until every hydraulic conduit was etched into her thoughts like a map of veins. She returned the pen to its rest without a sound.

Finch examined the signature, nodded in mild satisfaction, and drew an iron ring from a locked drawer beneath the desk. The ring bore a punched brass access badge and three notched bypass keys, heavy and square-toothed, filed to trip the emergency dogs inside the drainage tunnels.

"These admit you to the lower galleries, below the thirty-fathom mark," he said, sliding the keys across the blotter toward her. "The air down there is choked with damp rot, and the rock weeps day and night. Keep the bypass gates greased and the telltale pipes clear of silt."

"I will tend to the gates, Mr. Finch," she said, gathering the cold iron into her palm. She closed her fingers around the teeth of the keys, feeling the hard edges bite into her flesh like an old promise.

## Scene 3

The inspection gallery ran straight through the foot of the High Reach Dam, a narrow vault of cyclopean concrete bored three hundred feet below the crest where the weight of the impounded lake bore down through the rock. Moisture seeped through the contraction joints in slow, bitter beads, dripping into the gutter trench that lined the walkway. In the draft that pulled through the hollow heart of the wall, the hanging filament bulbs flickered and guttered against the sweating bulkhead like dying tallow dips.

Teresa pressed her thumb against the three notched square-toothed keys resting in her coat pocket. The grease-burn scars across her jaw tightened in the chill. Above her hummed the turbines, their vibration traveling into the marrow of her shins, but here at the deepest horizon lay the hydraulic works Arthur Finch had forgotten: the twin low-level sediment scour channels.

She halted before the cast-iron schematic mounted beside the conduit shafts. Tracing the raised conduits with a gloved finger, she verified what Finch's operational summaries had glossed over. The scour conduits—the heavy ground-sluices meant to blow accumulated mud from the lakebed—did not feed into the main concrete spillways or the high generating flumes. Instead, they plunged through their own dedicated subterranean culverts, bypassing the structural crest entirely and emptying directly into the lower gorge half a mile downstream.

Beside the inspection hatch hung an expanded-mesh locker, bolted to the wet ribbing of the wall. Within its cage, hooked to a rusted peg, hung the bronze manual spindle override key, its heavy cruciform head untouched by corrosion. She looked at it once, noting the turn of its locking bar, and stepped past.

Teresa spread the folded bathymetric survey over the casing of the eastern sluice valve. She drew a brass rule across the contour lines. The reservoir was an unequal trough: thirty fathoms deep against the masonry wall, but sloping upward along the sunken valley to where the northern shelf rose toward the old river road. The Blackwood farmstead lay on that shallow bench, submerged beneath only ten fathoms of silt-laden slack water.

She ran the discharge arithmetic with a blunt stub of chalk on the iron flange. A full discharge through the low-level scours would pass three thousand cubic feet per second through the belly of the foundation. At that volume, the drawdown would not be uniform; the scour drew hardest along the submerged riverbed, peeling the surface down across the shelves before the main body could replenish them. It would strip the reservoir's upper reach, laying the northern basin shelf bare under open air while leaving forty feet of water against the dam face. The immense mass-gravity barrier would remain stable, unbreached and unstrained, even as the valley floor above it was drained to stone.

The math was pitiless and exact. The dam would stand, but the hearth would dry.

Teresa wiped the chalk from the valve flange with the side of her sleeve, leaving only grey smudges on the black iron, and locked the gallery inspection hatch behind her.

The iron rungs of the access shaft gave way to the rubber-matted deck of the control room, where the damp subterranean air yielded to the dry, ozone-laced warmth of vacuum tubes and humming relay banks.

At the oiled walnut console, Kester Holt sat hunched beneath the green-shaded task lamp, a brass-nibbed ruling pen poised in his ink-stained fingers. He was a slight man with salt-bitten skin and the restless, hollow-eyed vigil of someone who spent his nights breathing sour air from rubber hoses. Spread across the desk before him was the hydro-acoustic drum log for the upper basin, its paper chart scarred by recent abrasions where the printed ink traces had been scraped clean with the flat of a scalpel.

On the edge of the console sat a brass depth-gauge housing, still beading with chilled lake water that dripped silently onto the linoleum.

"The drum recorder was calibrated to register drift timber, not an unauthorized tender boat riding above the drowned churchyard," Teresa said, stepping past the relay cabinets into the pool of lamp light.

Kester's hand jerked, the ruling pen digging a ragged black trench across the paper. He did not turn round immediately. His shoulders hitched toward his ears, rigid as wire under tension, before he slowly leaned back in his swivel chair. His gaze flicked over her maintenance overalls, down to the grease-burn scars along her jaw, and finally to the ring of heavy iron keys hanging at her hip.

"You should be clearing silt traps on the intake sills, Vane," he said, his voice scraped raw and pitched low against the hum of the turbines. "Nobody asked for an engineer up here before the midnight changeover."

"Nobody asked for silver communion plate from Saint Jude's to be hauled up forty feet of silt either," she said, nodding toward the oilskin bundle tucked beneath his stool, its edges wet and smelling of stagnant mud. "Finch will notice the pressure anomalies along the north cable array within two days. When the hydro-acoustic record shows a gap every Tuesday night, he won't look at the rivermen. He will look at the technician who signs off on the signal balance."

Kester stared at the scraped parchment, the color draining from his cheekbones. "What do you want? Finch signs the dismissal chits without a hearing. He'd have the state wardens waiting at the gate."

"I want the gallery motion sensors blinded," Teresa said, resting one calloused hand upon the edge of the walnut desk. "And the monitoring loop on the scour conduits pulled from the master annunciator panel during the early morning watch."

He squinted at her, his brow knitting in sharp suspicion. "The low-level conduits? What business does hydraulic maintenance have poking around the bottom valves off-shift? If those alarms flag an open circuit, the main board trips."

"You know how to jumper the relays so the board reads normal line resistance," Teresa replied, her tone level and unyielding. "A ten-cent copper bridge behind the telemetry rack. Keep your dives off the recorder, Holt, and I will never mention what comes up in your nets. Cut my path through the gallery, or we can lay that wet drum chart on Finch's desk before the morning whistle."

Kester swallowed, his gaze darting between the ruined log and the steady grey flint of her eyes. He wiped his palms on his trousers, then set the ruling pen down upon the desk with a faint, hollow clatter.

"The motion grid on the third level has a twelve-minute diagnostic blind spot every dawn," he muttered. "I can wire the scour loop into the bypass shunt. Nobody will see the circuit break."

"Tomorrow night," Teresa said, and turned back toward the shadows of the gallery hatch.

The iron rungs of the descent ladder smelled of graphite grease and seepage. Teresa climbed down into the High Reach gallery, three hundred feet beneath the crest, her boot heels striking the perforated steel decking with the dull resonance of lead dropped onto wet flagstones. Kester Holt descended behind her, his breath rasping through his teeth like paper dragged over gravel, his inspection lantern throwing cage-like shadows of ribbing across the vaulted concrete.

At the terminus of the eastern passage, where the curved wall thickened against the ballast of eighty fathoms of captured basin, the maintenance cage stood recessed into the rock. She reached into her canvas pocket and withdrew the ring holding three notched square-toothed keys. The center key turned stiffly within the brass padlock, biting through years of lime scale and grit until the latch bar sprang back with a hollow snap.

Inside, suspended between sets of heavy turnbuckles and lead packing irons, rested the bronze manual spindle key. It was a massive casting, nearly five feet in length and greened with patina, terminating in a massive cruciform head engineered to match the bevel drives of the lower scour valves. Teresa took its dead weight in both hands, lifting it free of the rusted cradle hooks. It pulled at her shoulders with the numbing drag of cold metal.

"Over here," she said, not looking back to see if he flinched.

Kester moved past her toward the pedestal where the manual bypass assembly rose from the gallery floor like an unadorned iron stump. He dropped to his knees in the film of stagnant wash, unbuckling a leather tool kit. With trembling fingers, he scooped thick graphite tallow from a tin, working the dark grease into the coarse teeth of the exposed worm gears, using a notched brass scraper to clear the salt crust that had cemented the bypass spindle during decades of disuse. The wet concrete wept steadily behind him, water trickling down the grout lines in cold ribbons that pooled around his trousers.

"The dogs are frozen," Kester muttered, wiping his forearm across his brow and leaving a black smear across his skin. "If Finch tries to trip the auxiliary solenoid from the control desk while we're turned in, the circuit won't even register a draw."

"Let him try," Teresa said.

She hoisted the bronze key, lifting the cruciform socket over the upright boss of the manual spindle. It seated with a heavy, dead clang that reverberated through the pressurized piping below the floor. Reaching to the wall rack, she pulled down the hollow steel lever bar, sliding its sleeve over the key's transverse arm to extend her purchase.

Teresa braced her boots against the raised lip of the conduit trench and threw her weight into the lever. The assembly groaned—a sound of tearing grit and stubborn iron—before the bypass shaft yielded a quarter turn. Then another. With a sudden, definitive drop, the mechanical pawl engaged the inner ratchet, seating the lock dogs into the carriage of the low scour gate. It was entirely independent of the electric relays that wired the gallery to the control room above. Once driven home, no switch at Arthur Finch's board could command the gate closed against the bronze wedge now holding the conduit open.

The mechanism was primed. Teresa did not dismantle the cheater bar, nor did she withdraw the key from the housing. She left the bronze spindle seated in the gut of the dam, waiting for the hour.

## Scene 6

Three miles downstream from the concrete breastwork, Oakhaven Settlement huddled against the river's lower elbow, a warren of clapboard houses and sod embankments built on ground that lived under the shadow of High Reach. In the wash of rain that fell across the parish clinic, the low eaves dripped into zinc barrels with the dull, hollow knocking of an iron clock.

Myra Vance—her cousin, who had scrubbed the lime from the valley's evicted barns twenty years ago only to bind wounds in this muddy hollow—wiped carbolic water from her red-knuckled fingers and drew the dispensary curtain shut. Outside, the single copper line that ran from the dam's powerhouses hummed against its porcelain insulators, carrying the steady current that kept the infant warmers humming in the back ward and the levee sump pumps clear of silt.

"You cannot turn the low valves, Teresa," Myra said, her voice dropping beneath the steady hiss of the steam kettle on the hearth. She did not raise her eyes to the grease-burn scars along Teresa's jawline; she knew every knot of flesh there without looking. "The head pressure on the turbines is the only thing keeping the lines alive. If you dump eighty fathoms into the riverbeds before summer, the clinic loses light, the refrigeration fails, and the dikes down at the bend will scour out like wet paper. There are four hundred souls between this porch and the marshes."

Teresa stood motionless by the window, her shoulder pressed to the rough casing. Through the mottled glass, the dark swelling bulk of the levee was visible against the grey river, an earthen shield thrown up to hold back what the masonry above held suspended. For a heartbeat, the air in the dispensary turned stifling and tight. She saw the iron cot where an asthmatic child drew raspy breaths beneath a glass misting tent; she smelled the clean liniments and the cold, fragile domesticity that had gathered in the basin's run-off. She could leave the cheater bar where it lay. She could slip down the road to the coast before dawn, leaving the sediment buried under sixty feet of slate water, leaving the drowned chimney of her father's house to rot in eternal dark. A quiet life was an easy thing to buy.

Yet behind her closed eyes rose the cold stone floor of the Blackwood kitchen, its hearth swept clean of cinders and drowned by decree while Arthur Finch's surveyors marked the elevations on their dry linen charts. That debt had not grown lighter with twenty winters of silence. It had only hardened into an obligation as unbending as pig iron.

"The pumps will find coal if they must," Teresa said, and the words fell like a dropped latch. "And the levees were built to hold six thousand feet if the engineers did their work. But the basin was stolen, Myra. The hearth stones were promised the sky again, and I will not leave them under Arthur Finch's pond to save his town from an unseasonable freshet."

Myra looked at her, the wet towel clenched in both hands until water beaded between her knuckles. She did not argue further. In the silence that settled between them, the hum of the wire overhead seemed thin, a fragile strand stretched across a deep and coming dark.

The road back up the gorge was a sluice of mountain gravel and gray foam, the rain having broken over the crest in a sudden, violent deluge that sheeted against the windscreen before she even reached the perimeter gate. By midnight, the storm had transformed every dry creek along the rim into a roaring tributary, hurling tons of mountain runoff down the gullets of the basin until the impounded water groaned behind the concrete face.

Teresa climbed the spiral iron stair to the control room, her oilskins dripping dark pools across the riser plates. The air inside smelled of ozone, wet wool, and the stale dregs of chicory coffee boiled black. Across the wide chamber, bathed in the jaundice of the dial panels, stood Arthur Finch, the man whose signature on the eviction parchment had buried her family’s valley two decades ago. His iron-gray hair, usually parted with clerical precision, hung in damp, frayed locks across a gray brow. His necktie was undone, the collar torn open at the hollow of his throat.

He did not turn when the heavy hatch sealed shut behind her. His knuckles were white against the brass levers of the auxiliary spillway governor, his boots braced against the oiled walnut console as though his own spine took the hydraulic thrust of the entire river.

"The telemetry out of High Ridge Gulch gave out twenty minutes ago," Arthur said, his voice flat, gravel-scraped, pitched beneath the rumble of the crest gates. "Four inches in ninety minutes. The upper basin is shedding every gallon it took in during the autumn freeze, and the pool is rising an inch every three minutes."

Teresa stepped forward, water seeping down the line of her collar, her fingers brushing the grease-burn scars along her jaw. On the master panel, the red telltale needles for the crest sector had crept past the amber warning mark into the hatch-lined danger band. The dam was holding back the weight of a black, drowning sea, vibrating so deeply that the teacup rattling in its saucer on the desk chimed like a struck bell. The concrete trembled under their boots.

"Open the central crest flumes," she said, her voice taut. "Dump the head. If the apron takes a hydraulic jump at this elevation, the backwash will scour the footing."

"If I trip the crest flumes now, the wave reaches the levee bend at Oakhaven in twelve minutes," he replied without looking away from the manometer tube, where the mercury column quivered and crept upward. "The bottom of the town will be under four feet of gravel wash before the siren finishes its first cycle. The hospital basement is twenty paces from the levee ditch. They don't have the pumps for it."

He reached out, his shoulder trembling with sheer fatigue, and hauled back on the third mechanical servo valve. Metal shrieked down the conduit lines. The deep, muffled shudder of a spillway gate rising two feet against the torrent vibrated up the bedrock, rattling the loose bolts in the window frame.

"You cannot throttle three rivers with eight men and a bank of bronze relays, Arthur," she said quietly.

"Then I throttle them alone," he snapped, but the anger was hollowed out, consumed by an exhaustion that made his hands shake as he notched the wheel a half-turn counterclockwise to trim the flow. "They sleep down there because they believe this wall was built by men who knew what they were doing. If I surrender the crest to ease the bulkhead, Oakhaven drowns to keep our ledger clean. I signed the warrants that drove sixty families off the meadows so this concrete could stand. I will not have them drowned by my negligence to spare the crown of the wall."

He leaned his forearms against the console, his head sagging between his shoulders, eyes bloodshot and fixed on the trembling needles. He was fifty paces from total collapse, his body propped up only by the mechanical resistance of the levers beneath his palms. For twenty years she had imagined him behind this desk as an unfeeling clerk of state power, a man who traded homesteads for megawatt tallies on ruled paper. Yet here he stood in the dead of night, broken-backed and alone, straining his frail heart against the deluge to shield the very town she was willing to place at risk.

She fled the console while his chin was still sunk against his collar, taking the emergency spirals three steps at a time until the heat of the generators died and the dead chill of the bedrock closed around her. Three hundred feet below the crest, the gallery stretched like a tomb bored through granite, its seepage troughs trickling with black seepage that smelled of lime and river rot. The bronze manual spindle override key stood where she and Kester had left it, its nearly five-foot shaft seated firmly into the bypass pedestal, the iron cheater bar wedged through its cruciform head like an executioner's lever. It was primed to trip the scour gates free of the master board above.

A boot struck the metal grating of the landing behind her, ragged and uneven.

Arthur Finch leaned into the gallery archway, one hand clamped against the wet rib of concrete, his chest heaving as if the descent had torn the lining of his lungs. His iron-gray hair was plastered to his forehead by cold spray and sweat, and in his right hand he carried an inspector's heavy brass torch, its beam shaking across the greasy floor until it struck the bronze key locked into the valve housing.

"Step away from the spindle," he rasped, his voice scraping raw in the narrow conduit. "If you break those bypass seals, the telemetry won't catch it in time. We are four inches from overtopping the parapet. You'll gut the basin."

Teresa did not drop her hands from the cheater bar. Her fingers tightened on the cold iron, knuckles whitening under the grease. "The telemetry is already blind, Arthur. Your instruments have been dead for two hours."

He squinted through the gloom, the beam of his torch lifting slowly from the mechanical linkage to the ridge of scars tracing her jawline. The tremor in his arm deepened. For weeks she had passed him on the turbine deck as nothing more than a quiet grease-monkey with a clipboard, an interchangeable hireling among the grease drums; now, in the silence of the foundation vault, the hollows of her face caught the glare, and the decades of administrative distance seemed to peel away from his eyes.

"Vane," he whispered, the name dropping like a lead sinker into the sump. "Caleb Vane's girl. You were ten years old when the bailiffs cleared the orchards."

"Eleven," she said. The word was flat, hard, devoid of the fury that had kept her awake through twenty winters of rented rooms and drafting tables. "And you signed the condemnation order with a gold pen on the hood of a county car while my mother was still loading the cookstove into the dray. You told us the valley had a higher purpose than pasture. You told us the state required the land for light."

Arthur took a half-step forward, not to seize the lever, but as though his knees could no longer bear the upright dignity of his office. He sank against the gallery wall, his shoulder smearing through a slick growth of green mold that wept from the masonry joints. 

"I signed it," he said, his breath hitching on the confession. "God help me, Teresa, I signed sixty-four of them in a single week. I told myself the grid would make men of us all, that the power would wash the shame out of the paper. But there has not been a night since the spillway closed that I haven't heard the river rising against those roofs. I know what was drowned. I know what was stolen."

He raised his head, his bloodshot eyes pleading through the murk.

"Drown me if you must," he whispered. "Take the key and crush my hands in the gearing. But downstream, in Oakhaven—they are running the levee sumps on the head pressure we bleed through the turbines. Myra's ward is full of children who will suffocate if the grid drops before dawn. If you throw the main floodgates, the back-surge will tear out the dikes before the sirens can sound. Settle it with me. Spare the town."

Teresa looked down at the cheater bar. The metal seemed to pulse beneath her skin, vibrating with the catastrophic kinetic energy gathered behind eighty fathoms of captured dark. If she wrenched the primary floodway bypass, the dam would disgorge its belly in a singular, ruinous fury: the concrete apron would crack, the valley below would be swept clear of every telegraph pole and levee, and Arthur Finch would spend the remainder of his broken days in a cell, ruined beside the ruin he had built. It was the total reckoning she had promised the hearth stones when the muddy water first crept across the threshold of her nursery.

Yet across the basin, beneath the storm-whipped foam, lay the shallow northern shelf. Her calculations had been exact. She had drafted them across dozens of late-shift grease-sheets: the bottom scour conduits had a discharge rate of three thousand cubic feet per second, an aperture sufficient to evacuate the siltered silt and peel the shallow waters off the old Blackwood homestead without dropping the reservoir's operating head or starving the generators that kept Oakhaven's levee pumps alive.

Vengeance was an appetite that demanded the whole world drown to prove a wound. Justice was an engineering problem.

Teresa braced both boots against the flange of the bypass housing and set her shoulder beneath the extension bar. 

"Keep your town," she said.

She heaved upward with every ounce of muscle she owned. The bronze manual spindle shrieked as its square threads engaged, biting through twenty years of hardened tallow and oxide. Metal groaned against metal throughout the depth of the bulkhead. With a dull, reverberating crack that shook the chalk from the gallery ceiling, the mechanical dog clicked home, locking the sediment scour valve into its open aperture. Deep within the bedrock beneath their boots, three hundred feet beneath the foaming dark, the scour sluice roared to life, sucking three thousand cubic feet of mud and river silt each second into the deep diversion culverts. The shelf above Blackwood farm began to drain.

Teresa let go of the cheater bar, her chest burning, and turned toward the north gallery stair to watch the drowned hearth rise from the mud.

## Scene 9

The iron rungs gave way to daylight that felt thin and cold against Teresa's face, unspooling across the raw rim of the Blackwood Valley Basin where ten fathoms of drowned quiet had smothered her family's orchard for twenty years. What remained below was not water, but an expanse of slate-colored sludge that stretched between the pine-crested ridges like a fresh scar.

She walked down the shelving bank, her work boots sinking instantly past the welt into cold, greasy silt that tugged with each step as though the riverbed were reluctant to surrender anything it had once swallowed. The mud smelled of choked reeds, dead river-mussel shells, and the curdled mineral reek of twenty winters sealed off from the sky. Every footfall made a wet, sucking sound that broke the stillness and died without an echo. Nothing lived down here. No birds had dropped into the trough yet, and the wind merely skated over the flats, flattening pools of sulfurous run-off into greasy sheets.

Behind her, the low shuffle of boots labored in the muck. Arthur had followed down the spillway slope, his iron-gray head bowed against the wind, clutching his torn coat at the throat with fingers stiffened by the frost and the long night at the windlass. He did not call out to her. He only kept the distance of a man following an executioner across a leveled field, each ragged breath rattling faintly in his chest as he dragged himself through the grey sludge.

Near the center of the terrace, the mud fell away into a low mound where the silt had washed thinner over sunken rubble. Teresa climbed the rise, her knees aching, her breath clouding pale in the dawn chill.

There it was.

The timber frame of the farmhouse had rotted into black pulp two decades ago, swept into the deep scour or dissolved into the lake bottom, leaving behind only the squared foundation stones and the fieldstone chimney that Caleb Vane had laid with river lime and a mason’s trowel. The hearth stones sat exposed beneath the naked sky, drying in the bitter autumn air, gray and pitted where the scour currents had eaten the mortar from between their seams. For twenty years she had carried the memory of that hearth like an iron plate inside her ribs, an oath whispered into pillowcases and engine bilges: that the reservoir would be forced to return what it stole, that she would not rest until the hearth stood dry under open sun again.

She stood before it now.

The stones were dry. The sun struck them flat and pale, illuminating the chipped corner of the hearth-seat where her father had once tapped out his clay pipes, and the iron pot-crane, still pinned in its socket, encrusted in freshwater calc and lime until it resembled a withered, fossilized limb. There was no ghost waiting for her in the chimney flue. There was no recovered childhood resting upon the hearth-seat, no warmth preserved beneath the silt, no restitution in the dead rock. The valley was only a ditch scraped raw by machinery and choked with twenty years of sediment. The hearth was just five slabs of gray river granite resting in the mud.

Arthur stopped three paces behind her. He did not offer an excuse, nor did he ask for mercy; he stood with his mud-caked trousers dripping onto the stones, his hands hanging limp at his sides, looking down at the empty lintel as if reading the names of everyone he had evicted from the bottomland.

"The town downstream is safe," he said quietly, his voice cracked with exhaustion.

Teresa did not look back at him. She looked once more at the dry stones, feeling the fierce, scorching fire that had driven her across half a continent gutter down to cold ash, leaving behind only an immense, hollow quiet. The vow was kept, and it had yielded nothing.

She turned her back on the hearth and began the slow climb out of the mud toward the valley road.
