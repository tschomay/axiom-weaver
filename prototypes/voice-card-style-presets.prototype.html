The Voice Card and the style presets

Question this demo answers: what fields make up the Voice Card, do style presets expanded from those fields actually produce genuinely different prose from identical Scene Card input, how does an author override one field without unpicking the rest, how does a Voice Card become prompt text, how does a scene's own tone compose with it, and what a later continuity pass could compare drift against.

Identical input across every preset below: Cinderella fixture, scene_01_remarriage — entry state Cinderella at loc_house, exit state at loc_chimney_corner, tone "wistful, quietly bleak." Three required beats: the remarriage, the stepdaughters' haughtiness, the housework/chimney-corner demotion. Each paragraph below was generated by hand against the rendering template in Prompt rendering, standing in for the writer call — this prototype has no live model wiring, the repo has no app yet to wire one into.

Prompt rendering

The Voice Card renders as a fixed block, separate from the scene's own tone instruction, so the writer never reads tone as permission to change register:

VOICE (story-level, stable across the whole telling):
- Point of view: {person}, {tense} tense. Narrative distance: {narrative_distance}.
- Register: {register}
- Sentence rhythm: {sentence_rhythm}
- Preferred imagery (draw from these before inventing new ones): {imagery_palette}
- Dialogue: {dialogue_density}
- Exemplar, for calibration only — do not reuse its content: "{style_exemplar}"   [omitted if empty]

SCENE TONE (this scene only — shapes emphasis and imagery selection, never overrides the voice above):
{scene.tone}

A flat attribute list ("register: dark, elevated") reads as description, not instruction, if that's all a writer gets — the difference in the five samples above came from pairing each field with concrete texture (imagery domains, a rhythm rule with an example), not from the field labels themselves. That's a finding for the writer-prompt-contract ticket (issue #16) as much as this one.

Field-level override

Fairy-Tale/Fable, register changed from "plain, warmly ironic, folk-narrator; understates cruelty and wonder alike" to "plain, dry, faintly sardonic — the narrator no longer softens the cruelty with warmth." Every other field held identical.

There was once a gentleman who married, for his second wife, the proudest and haughtiest woman that ever was seen. She brought two daughters exactly like her, and within the week the household had sorted itself the way such households always do: three idle, one working. Cinderella did not complain. There was no one to complain to.

Storage

A Voice Card is always fully materialized — never a sparse diff against the preset. Selecting a preset copies all nine fields in; editing one field edits that field only. The record keeps based_on: "fairy-tale-fable" purely so the UI can show "modified from Fairy-Tale/Fable" and offer a reset — it plays no role in prompt rendering, which only ever reads the materialized fields. Rationale: a sparse diff would need the preset's own definition to resolve the unset fields at render time, and if that shared definition is edited later every story built on it silently changes voice mid-telling. Full materialization freezes the card at the moment it was chosen. Naming: "{preset display name} (modified)" until the author renames it.

Per-scene modulation

Rule: the five identity fields (person, tense, distance, register, rhythm) are near-invariant for the whole telling. tone lives on the Scene Card and governs imagery selection and emphasis only — expressed as a separate instruction line, never merged into the voice block, so it can't be read as license to change register scene to scene.

this scene · wistful, quietly bleak
She slept, not in a chamber with a soft bed, but in the chimney-corner among the cinders, and it was from this that her sisters, in mockery, first called her Cinderella.
hypothetical later scene · triumphant, dazzled
She stood at the top of the stair in gold and silver brocade, and for one candlelit moment the whole glittering court turned to look, and nobody in the room remembered the cinders at all.

Same person/tense/register/rhythm (Fairy-Tale/Fable) in both — short declaratives, the folk-narrator's plain warmth. What moves is which items from imagery_palette get pulled forward: ash and cinders for the bleak scene, gold/brocade/candlelight for the dazzled one. Tone selects from the palette; it doesn't replace the voice that's rendering it.

Drift measurement

Proposal for what the continuity pass (issue #15) would compare a later scene against:

No live prose to anchor against yet at scene 1. Compare directly to style_exemplar if present — that's the only ground truth that exists before anything has been generated.
From scene 2 on, anchor against measured prose, not the card's text description. After the first one or two scenes generate, extract a small set of cheap stats from their prose/digest — average sentence length, dialogue fraction, which imagery_palette domains actually appear in the Scene Digest's imagery_signature — and store that as the story's measured voice anchor. A description like "short declarative sentences" is a target; the first two scenes' actual numbers are the baseline that later scenes get compared to.
Flag, don't gate. A later scene whose stats fall outside a band around the anchor (e.g. average sentence length drifting >40%) surfaces to the continuity pass as a candidate seam, the same way a broken plant obligation would — it doesn't block the unattended read-time run.

Findings against the ticket's questions

All eight fields earned their place on this scene except one is unconfirmed: dialogue_density couldn't be exercised because scene_01_remarriage has no dialogue beats. Kept in the schema — the seam-failure rubric names character-voice homogenization as dialogue-sensitive — but it needs a dialogue-heavy scene to actually validate. Flagged as unconfirmed rather than dropped.
Five presets read as five different narrators from identical beats/entry/exit/tone: fairy-tale warmth, gothic dread, comic patter, hardboiled economy, and literary interiority are distinguishable without needing the preset name attached. Sentence rhythm and imagery palette did the most visible work; person and tense stayed constant across all five (third/past) so this scene doesn't test their effect — worth a second pass with a first-person or present-tense preset before calling the field set complete.
